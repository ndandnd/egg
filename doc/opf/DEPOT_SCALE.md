# A depot large enough to affect the feeder

Use one operator with two overlapping service batches, a common pool of whole buses and multiple identical depot plugs for the first scaled experiment. Choose service counts and electrical ratings before observing prices, outcomes or solve times. Keep the full timetable MILP as a small-case cross-check and a later generalization with its own budget. The historical clean A2 method certified 64/64 affine-price cases, but those had 8 or 12 trips. Its median 24 fleet-oracle calls and 38.48 seconds are evidence about that range only. The public 37-service certificate used different, synthetic supply and custom proof bounds. Its positive gap cannot be transferred to an OPF. ## Exact physical alternatives in the scaled model

Let all morning services use battery energy a and afternoon services use b, each within usable capacity B. The buses start and end full, have common efficiency η, no auxiliary load, and return to one depot for the entire early and terminal windows. Each serves at most one service per batch. Any allowed morning–afternoon pair incurs zero transition time and energy. The actual allowed graph must have a witnessed matching of size ν. Its submatchings witness every smaller cardinality. A plan with m pairs has nA−m morning-only buses, nB−m afternoon-only buses and m paired buses. It pays a fixed fee per used bus. Preserve the matching and a bus-level charging witness. Identical energies within a batch make the choice of which m pairs immaterial under these assumptions.

Let a particular bus draw A in the morning and D in the afternoon, assigning zero to an idle batch. Its total grid purchase is s=(A+D)/η. In a window w of h_w hours a bus can accept at most p_w kW throughout. Its early purchase e in grid kWh must satisfy

    max(0, (A+D−B)/η, s−p_late h_late) ≤ e ≤ min(A/η, p_early h_early).

The lower bounds protect afternoon departure and terminal replenishment. The upper bound prevents early overcharging. An empty interval rules out that matching size. Add the individual bounds for the three bus types with their multiplicities. With C_w plugs and site limit G_w kW, use K_w=h_w min(C_w p_w, G_w). Intersect the early-total interval with [S−K_late, K_early], where S is the sum of s across buses. Independently enforce any fleet cap. A posted-price optimum comes from comparing the interval endpoints plus the vehicle fee for every feasible m. At a zero spread the entire interval ties on energy. Keep the complete set of distinct optimal loads for diagnostics even if the implemented rule selects just one.

**Why the interval really can be charged.** Any total in the sum of individual intervals can be disaggregated by filling from the lower bounds to that total. In either window let u_i be each bus's allocated energy divided by h_w and let y be their sum. Vectors with that sum and 0 ≤ u_i ≤ p_w form a polytope. At each vertex at most one coordinate lies strictly inside its bounds (otherwise transfer a small amount between two such coordinates). Thus at most ceil(y/p_w) plugs are needed at any vertex, at most C_w. Express the desired vector as a convex combination of these vertices and use each for the corresponding fraction of the common window. The instantaneous total stays y throughout, including under the site limit. Each individual SOC is monotone during charging. This construction assumes controllable power, interruptible charging with arbitrary switching and zero setup time.

This extends the draft's single-connector reasoning to a homogeneous shared charger pool. A single 3 MW plug would falsely let one bus accept 3 MW. Use the pooled construction rather than multiplying the charge of one small fleet. For each m the physical optimum minimizes the grid value along its continuous interval. Compare all such optima for D and use the hull of the joint energy–fee endpoints for H. Preserve continuous interpolation **within** one interval as a physical action and interpolation across fleet counts as a candidate convexification. A small independent bus-indexed assignment/charging MILP and AC replay should check both the reconstructed physical schedules and costs before scaling. A second conventional control should select a deterministic minimum-fleet cover first, optimize charging for it under the identical feeder, and retain all infeasible or failed cases. An unconstrained load-only battery relaxation cannot certify a whole-bus schedule.

## Electrical scale and test for an uninformative loop

Set a depot's ex-ante nameplate ratio to min(installed-plug power, site connection power) divided by a **named, fixed** transformer or line rating. Convert MVA to its active-power equivalent under the declared power-factor convention. For example, if the documented interface is 5 MVA at unity power factor, 4, 16 or 32 plugs at 125 kW each give 0.5, 2 or 4 MW and ratios 0.10, 0.40 or 0.80, conditional on a sufficient site cap. Those figures are illustrative. A missing nameplate rating, particularly the unspecified limits of the original 33-bus case, cannot supply a denominator. Make the fleet large enough with actual additional overlapping services and whole buses, selecting counts, draws, usable batteries (e.g. 300–400 kWh), timing, plugs and separation policy before solves. Report both feasible peak draw and realized peak import, plus background loading, voltage, congestion and whether the chosen interface binds. The ratio itself measures neither spare capacity nor price impact.

Independently check the grid price response before interpreting cycles. Across the feasible early-energy range compare the early-minus-late depot price spread (EUR/MWh), including one-sided values, with the fleet's exact switching prices. For two windows the grid value restricted to (E,S−E) is convex in E, so its subgradients are ordered where feasible. Certified endpoint spread bounds can bound the intervening response. If the attainable spread range admits just one common best-response load under the registered price and fleet selection rules, there is no fleet switching margin for the loop to exploit in that case. Preserve the case. A numerically small spread range alone can straddle a switching price. If bounds or boundary prices are indeterminate, report them as such. Freeze the tolerance for economically material spread changes and all response/tie rules before comparing methods.

Each window must coincide with one OPF price/profile interval, or be split with a corresponding rederivation. Averaging several real-price hours can change the optimum. Bidirectional exchange with losses, staggered depot returns, heterogeneous charger eligibility, nonpreemptive sessions and extra service batches require a different feasibility argument. Charge-only results make no signed V2G claim. The current operator and feeder sources determine modeling assumptions, not a verified physical pairing of a German operator and depot.

## Sizing on the downloaded rural feeder

The native switch configuration gives 91 eligible downstream 20 kV load-bus classes. We selected the shortest and longest energized line distances, with original bus identifier breaking ties. We used no prices, load/PV profiles or solved voltages to choose either bus. The nearest is MV1.101 Bus 78 at 0.15 km. Its path crosses a 7.621 MVA line. The farthest is MV1.101 Bus 68 at 22.3 km. Its weakest lines have a 9.803 MVA rating. The two 25 MVA source transformers are electrically parallel in this configuration. Their combined rating would overstate both path ratings. The equipment paths and limits are recorded in [the topology receipt](../../result/opf_qualification/repair02/depot_paths.json). It contains information from the SimBench database, available under the [Open Database License](https://simbench.de/en/download/).

For an engineering design anchored to those paths, round each target to the nearest whole 125 kW plug. Give each window the same number of plugs and set the site cap to their combined power. Each batch contains ceil(0.8 × plugs) required services. Each bus has a 350 kWh battery with 30 kWh reserved. The morning and afternoon services use 140 and 90 battery kWh respectively. Either one-hour charging window permits 95%-efficient charging, with unity power factor. Pair eligibility is complete in this design. The two ranges of whole-bus counts below reflect how many services share a bus. Their endpoints are feasible fleet covers under these assumptions. ### How much price movement could change a duty?

These six designs give a useful scale check before solving the network. With one hour and a 125 kW charger, a morning-only bus needs at least 140/0.95 − 125 early grid kWh to finish full. A paired bus needs at least (140+90)/0.95 − 125. An afternoon-only bus needs no early charge. Splitting one pair into two buses can therefore move at most 90/0.95 = 94.737 kWh from early to late. The shared terminal cap can only reduce that amount. Every cover has the same upper bound on early energy because each morning bus, paired or single, can take 125 early kWh.

Let f be the incremental daily cost of using an extra bus in EUR. If the early-minus-late price spread is positive, lowering early energy by 94.737 kWh can save at most (90/0.95) × spread / 1000 EUR. Consequently a spread strictly below 1000 f × 0.95 / 90 cannot justify even one extra bus. Nonpositive spreads also favor the minimum-bus cover. In these six designs the terminal cap leaves room for the first split to realize the full 94.737 kWh: for n services per batch and C plugs it requires (C−n) × 125 ≥ 90/0.95, and each design has C−n ≥ 1. At equality a minimum-bus and a larger cover tie. Using the draft's **scenario** fees of €50 and €100 per extra bus-day gives first switches at €527.78 and €1,055.56/MWh, respectively. Sunk vehicles or different incremental staffing costs would change f. The numbers are sensitivity examples. Whether the feeder reaches either spread is unmeasured. A fleet can still move charging between windows without changing any duty if the spread changes sign. Report the two decisions separately.
