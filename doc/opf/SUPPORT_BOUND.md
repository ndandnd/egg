# What a binding grid limit can tell us about whole buses

Let E denote early AC kWh, S total AC kWh to operate the services, and m the number of morning–afternoon pairs. The fleet uses N−m buses at fee f each. Suppose the feasible early loads at each of a consecutive set of integer counts are intervals [l_m,U] with a common upper endpoint, and l_m is nondecreasing and discrete convex. Profile, grid, and recourse costs depend on E through the two prescribed power shapes; write their sum as G(E). G can include hard infeasibility. Define D as the best physical cost and H as the best cost when the fleet may mix whole schedules and the grid serves the mean load. Both use the same G. Mixed schedules do not describe buses actually operated together.

## Rounding a hull solution

Linearly interpolate l_m between neighboring counts to obtain l(t). Discrete convexity makes this interpolant convex. Any mixture at mean count t must have E ≥ l(t) by Jensen's inequality and E ≤ U. Conversely, mixing schedules in the two neighboring count layers fills every point from l(t) to U. This gives the exact hull in the (count, energy) plane; the fee is already affine in count.

Take a finite attained hull solution (t,E). Rounding down to floor(t) retains precisely the same E, since l_floor(t) ≤ l(t) ≤ E ≤ U. Hence all grid flows, voltage limits, alternative supply and its cost can be retained. Only the fee increases. Thus
`0 ≤ D−H ≤ f(t−floor(t)) < f`. An integer hull witness gives zero. This is a bound for this entire two-window problem, independent of fleet size. Its proof does not justify multiplying a fee by the number of active voltage or thermal constraints, nor extending it to arbitrary multidimensional duty systems.

The homogeneous lower endpoint is the maximum of the shared late-capacity floor and an affine function of m. In the one-hour synthetic fixture, the individual bound rises by 90/0.95 kWh per pair only until the shared floor masks an increment when reading from high to low m. For heterogeneous runs, feasible edges enforce both bus windows and battery capacity. Additional forced early charge is a nonnegative edge cost over the unpaired lower requirements. Fixed-cardinality min-cost bipartite flow gives its minimum. The matching LP with fixed integral flow has an integral solution. Its value as a function of flow is convex by mixing feasible flows. The shared floor preserves convexity, and nested feasible edge submatchings give monotonicity here. The upper endpoint depends on morning runs alone. The implementation verifies these layer hypotheses before using the bound. For convex G, minimizing over E conditional on a real count t gives a convex value function. The minimum over integer counts therefore occurs at a neighbor of an optimal t. This permits an exact discrete reduction; numerical solves still need their own checks.

## When does a hard cap force a positive gap?

A sufficient example has two adjacent exposed count layers, an upper bound E ≤ C strictly between their lower endpoints and a unique relaxed optimum at C with a strict physical-versus-hull fee difference there. Then every physical point at C costs more, and every other E has strictly larger relaxed cost. For a compact fleet and lower-semicontinuous costs this gives D>H. A cap's location alone supplies neither uniqueness nor a mixed optimum. For example, take layers with fees 1 on [0,1] and 0 at 1, C=1/2 and G(E)=2E on the feasible interval. Both planners choose E=0 and pay 1. The cap lies strictly between forced levels. With G(E)=E, every feasible E minimizes the hull objective, the cap binds at one optimal hull point, and physical E=0 still gives D=H=1. There is no frequency claim without a sampling model for the cap.

A smooth feeder can give a strictly positive gap at a unique price. For the same two layers with G(E)=E² and no binding grid constraint, the physical optimum costs 1. The hull pays E²+1−E, minimized at E=1/2 with cost 3/4. Its marginal price is uniquely 1. Conversely, a zero-gap example with strict alternating responses at unique smooth prices appears in the earlier novelty review. These counterexamples separate support, price multiplicity and update dynamics. This comparison does not infer a cycle by observing a gap.

At the upper boundary C of fixed-load grid feasibility, the convex grid subgradient can contain the outward normal ray [G'₋(C),∞). An outward perturbation is infeasible; there need not be two finite marginal prices. Clearing a fractional fleet may select the fleet break-even spread from that set. This does not set the fixed-load grid's derivative or imply multiple equilibrium prices. If a unity-power-factor generator at the same bus is dispatched strictly inside its power bounds at a constant marginal cost, its stationarity condition pins the active-power price to that cost. At zero or at maximum output the equality need not hold. Even without an active voltage or thermal limit, resistive marginal losses can move the depot price away from the wholesale price.

The constant is sharp. Let one morning and one afternoon trip each draw 1 kWh, usable battery 1, efficiency 1, early per-bus limit 1 kWh and two terminal plugs with enough total capacity. Pairing forces E=1. Two singleton buses admit [0,1]. Subtract the common fee of one bus. Set grid cost to zero up to the independent early cap 1−ε and infeasible above it, with 0<ε<1. D=f, H=fε and the gap tends to f from below. In the relaxed optimum the fleet selects a break-even multiplier from the grid's normal cone. This optimum is a market clearing calculation, not an observed price orbit.

In both experimental fleet classes a *single price-independent minimum-floor matching for each cardinality* spans the entire feasible interval at that count. Even if a committed tie loses money, the experiment cannot show that the identity of a pair must be discovered from DLMPs. The feeder can affect which cardinality and which charging profile to select. The spread in EUR/MWh divided by 1000 enters G'(E) when E is in kWh.

**Equality without solving the grid.** Suppose the first and last feasible counts have the same lower endpoint. By monotonicity every layer then has the identical interval [l,U]. At every attainable E the maximum-count matching supplies the cheapest possible fleet fee. Both objectives consequently equal f(N−m_max)+inf_{E∈[l,U]} G(E). If a feasible finite grid dispatch exists, the gap is exactly zero; if none exists, both problems are infeasible and their difference is undefined. With unequal floors, the same argument proves equality at any particular load above the maximum-count floor. Global equality additionally requires showing that a hull optimum is attained at such a load.
