# Route and Response Learning in Price-Coupled EV Systems

**Full-text evidence notes — 2026-08-14**

## Scope and evidence note

This note consolidates a close reading of six locally supplied full-text papers that bear directly on endogenous electricity prices, vehicle routing or scheduling, coupled transport-power equilibria, economic incentives, and learned charging responses. The review is intentionally claim-oriented: for each paper it records what the paper actually establishes, its assumptions and numerical evidence, which broad novelty claims it makes unavailable, and the smallest defensible EVSP/learning/economic gap left open.

The evidence base is the following local PDFs only:

1. `lit reviews/1-s2.0-S0306261925005549-main.pdf` — Xie and Xu (2025).
2. `lit reviews/main2.pdf` — Ding, Li, and Jian (2022).
3. `lit reviews/main1.pdf` — Subramanian et al. (2022).
4. `lit reviews/Learning_the_Price_Response_of_Spatiotemporal_EV_Charging_Flow_A_Graph-Attentive_Surrogate_Model.pdf` — Chen et al. (2026).
5. `lit reviews/Network_Equilibrium_of_Coupled_Transportation_and_Power_Distribution_Systems.pdf` — Wei et al. (2018).
6. `lit reviews/12.pdf` — Song et al. (2025).

No web search or secondary summary was used for the substantive claims below. Page references are to the PDF/article pages visible in the supplied files. “Novelty killed” is deliberately conservative: it means a generic claim should not be made without a narrower qualifier; it does not imply that every adjacent variant has been exhausted.

## Executive synthesis

These papers collectively establish that all of the following generic ideas already exist:

- learning a strategic asset’s decision-dependent price curve from demand and clearing-price data;
- alternating routes or charging load with OPF/LMP updates until a fixed point;
- bilevel fleet routing and charging with route-induced nodal prices;
- dynamic coupled transport-power equilibrium with charging queues and road spillback;
- Nash and Stackelberg pricing between a mobility platform and a distribution-network operator; and
- a graph-attentive neural surrogate mapping price/service-fee and traffic conditions to spatiotemporal charging flow.

Accordingly, neither “the chicken-and-egg price iteration” nor “machine learning of routes from prices” is sufficient as a thesis contribution by itself.

The remaining coherent thesis-scale direction is:

> **Certified learning and economic design for a finite, scheduled EV fleet acting as a price-making flexible load.**

Its distinguishing combination is:

- mandatory service trips and discrete vehicle-duty covers rather than nonatomic traveler flows or independent OD routes;
- SOC-, time-, and charger-feasible EVSP best responses generated by column generation;
- endogenous market prices learned from limited historical information or returned by a grid-clearing oracle;
- explicit separation of price-taking response, strategic fleet payment, and social-generation-cost objectives;
- Nash, Stackelberg, or incentive-contract regimes grounded in the scheduled fleet’s private operational feasibility;
- learned grid and fleet response surrogates with active learning and out-of-distribution safeguards; and
- exact or discretized final correction/certification after approximate equilibrium search.

## 1. Xie and Xu (2025): strategic storage bidding with learned price-quota curves

**Citation.** Dunjian Xie and Yan Xu, “Strategic bidding of price-maker energy storage systems in electricity markets with limited information,” *Applied Energy* 390 (2025), 125824. DOI: 10.1016/j.apenergy.2025.125824.

### Actual application and model

The application is a 120 MW/120 MWh energy-storage system bidding every half hour in Singapore’s National Electricity Market. It jointly allocates capacity across energy, regulation, primary reserve, and contingency reserve. The storage operator is one strategic price maker facing limited disclosure: total market demand and product clearing prices are available, while grid topology, zonal demand, and rival bid stacks are not.

At interval \(t\), the offer vector is

\[
x_t=[P_t^E,P_t^{RC},P_t^{PR},P_t^{CR}]^\top,
\]

where \(P_t^E=-P_t^{buy}+P_t^{sell}\). The clearing-price vector

\[
\lambda_t=[\lambda_t^E,\lambda_t^{RC},\lambda_t^{PR},\lambda_t^{CR}]^\top
\]

is unknown when bids are submitted and depends on \(x_t\), making it decision-dependent uncertainty. The objective maximizes expected market income less battery-throughput degradation and O&M. Constraints cover product-capacity allocation, energy sufficient for full ancillary-service activation, SOC dynamics and limits, expected reserve deployment, and regulation energy consumption. See Sections 3–4.1, pp. 3–4.

The degradation model is a linear Ah/Wh-throughput approximation. The model contains no network congestion, rival adaptation, offer-price decision, or cross-product market-clearing model.

### SPQC and EPQC learning

For each product \(\diamond\), the authors estimate the joint density

\[
f_t^\diamond(\lambda_t^\diamond,u_t^\diamond-x_t^\diamond,z_t^\diamond),
\]

where \(u_t^\diamond\) is total market demand, \(x_t^\diamond\) is the ESS’s accepted zero-price offer, \(u_t^\diamond-x_t^\diamond\) is residual demand, and \(z_t^\diamond\) contains other conditions. The historical data are partitioned by half-hour time point, weekday/weekend, and public-holiday or regular-event condition. Weather, maintenance, and other market information are mentioned as possible covariates but are not developed empirically.

The joint density is estimated nonparametrically, with kernel density estimation offered as the example. Conditioning on a demand scenario and decision gives the stochastic price-quota curve (SPQC):

\[
f_t^\diamond(\lambda_t^\diamond\mid \tilde u_t^\diamond-x_t^\diamond,z_t^\diamond)
=
\frac{f_t^\diamond(\lambda_t^\diamond,\tilde u_t^\diamond-x_t^\diamond,z_t^\diamond)}
{\int f_t^\diamond(\lambda_t^\diamond,\tilde u_t^\diamond-x_t^\diamond,z_t^\diamond)d\lambda_t^\diamond}.
\]

Its conditional expectation is the expected price-quota curve (EPQC),

\[
g_{t,k}^\diamond(x_t^\diamond)=E[\lambda_{t,k}^\diamond(x_t^\diamond)].
\]

Traditional PQCs use rival bid stacks and are deterministic and stepwise. The SPQC represents a price distribution inferred from historical clearing points; the EPQC is its smoother conditional mean. See Section 4.2, pp. 4–5, and Figures 3–4, p. 8.

### Decision-dependent uncertainty and scenarios

Market demand is treated as exogenous and represented by \(K\) scenarios. Price remains conditional on both demand and the strategic decision. The authors recommend ordinary Monte Carlo or low-discrepancy lattice sampling, with optional aggregation or scenario reduction. The deterministic equivalent is

\[
\max \sum_k\sum_t \beta_k\left[g_{t,k}(x_t)^\top A x_t+B s_t\right].
\]

Replacing random price by its conditional expectation is compatible with their risk-neutral expected-revenue objective because quantity is fixed within a scenario and revenue is linear in price. It would not preserve price-distribution information for variance, chance constraints, or CVaR. Product-specific conditional densities are described, but preservation of cross-product price dependence is not explained. See Section 4.3, p. 5.

### Trust-region iterative algorithm

At iteration \(l\), each EPQC is locally linearized:

\[
g(x)\approx g(x^{(l)})+\phi^{(l)}(x-x^{(l)}),
\qquad
\phi^{(l)}\approx \frac{g(x^{(l)}+\Delta x)-g(x^{(l)})}{\Delta x}.
\]

A box trust region imposes \(|x-x^{(l)}|\le r^{(l)}\). An error ratio compares the true EPQC at the candidate point with the local prediction:

- if \(|\sigma-1|>0.2\), reject the whole candidate and halve the corresponding radius;
- if \(|\sigma-1|\le0.01\), accept and double the radius;
- otherwise accept without changing the radius.

Substituting an affine price response into price times quantity yields a QP. For a zero-price storage supply offer, more accepted supply should lower price, so the authors require non-increasing EPQCs, \(\phi\le0\), which makes the maximization QP convex. Outliers are removed and locally increasing segments are smoothed until monotonicity holds, but the procedure is not specified. The stopping tolerance is 0.01%; the displayed relative-objective test is not absolute and implicitly relies on monotone accepted progress. Global optimality is explicitly not guaranteed. Typical convergence is reported in 4–8 iterations. See Sections 4.4–4.5, pp. 5–7, and Figure 1, p. 7.

### Empirical setting and numerical results

The historical input is 2022–2023 NEMS/EMC total demand and clearing prices for the four products. The case assumes:

- 120 MW/120 MWh ESS;
- 80% cycling efficiency and charge/discharge efficiency approximately 0.89;
- zero self-discharge;
- 10,000 full-depth cycles;
- replacement cost 0.45 SGD/Wh;
- regulation energy use of 13%;
- primary- and contingency-reserve deployment probabilities of 0.2%; and
- 48 half-hour intervals per day.

In a typical-weekday test, the price-taker policy expects 109.90 kSGD but realizes an average 40.23 kSGD over 1,000 sampled PQCs, an error of −63.39%. The proposed price-maker policy expects 75.85 kSGD and realizes 76.11 kSGD, +0.34%. Table 1 contains two apparent publication defects: the prose gives 63.73% rather than the 63.39% implied by its values, and the reported “Min” values exceed the averages while the “Max” values are below them, strongly suggesting swapped labels. See Section 5.3 and Table 1, p. 9.

The yearly test runs 1 January–31 December 2023, with a rolling previous-six-month training window matched by day type and half-hour. Results in Table 2, p. 10, are:

| Case | Expected income (MSGD) | Simulated income (MSGD) | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Proposed nonlinear EPQC | 27.66 | 26.86 | −3.03% |
| Linear fitted PQC | 29.95 | 26.40 | −11.85% |
| Price taker | 41.83 | 15.32 | −63.36% |
| Perfect-information supply curve | 28.36 | 28.36 | 0% |

The proposed policy is approximately 5.29% below the perfect-information comparator. Raising efficiency from 80% to 90% raises annual income only 3.9%, because ancillary-service revenue dominates. Expanding from 50/50 to 250/250 MW/MWh raises modeled profit 248.8%, whereas 300/300 to 500/500 raises it 18.4%. The suggested “below 300 MW/300 MWh” size is simulation-specific, not a full investment optimum. See Sections 5.4–5.5, pp. 9–10.

### Assumptions and limitations

- ESS offers are priced at zero and assumed fully accepted; offer price itself is not strategic.
- The historical conditional density is treated as an intervention response. Historical demand–price association does not causally identify the counterfactual price impact of inserting a new large asset.
- “Realized” PQCs are sampled from the same estimated joint densities, so validation is primarily internal statistical consistency rather than an observed 120 MW market intervention.
- The model has one strategic asset, no Nash or Stackelberg rivals, no network, and no congestion.
- It is risk neutral and does not preserve multivariate price risk.
- The trust-region method is local; dominance over 1,000 random feasible bids is not a global certificate.
- No code or full reproducibility package is supplied. Missing details include kernel and bandwidth, sparse-cell treatment, outlier thresholds, monotone smoothing, scenario count and lattice construction, finite-difference step, initialization, QP solver, random seed, and compute time.

### Novelty killed

The broad claim “first to learn a decision-dependent price curve from limited public demand/price data and optimize a strategic flexible asset against it” is unavailable. The trust-region EPQC iteration is also an established starting method.

### Atomic EVSP/learning/economic gap

For an EV fleet as a strategic buyer, the sign reverses. With base system load \(U_{t,k}\) and fleet charging load \(L_t\), the natural response is

\[
g_{t,k}(L_t)=E[p_t\mid U_{t,k}+L_t,z_t],\qquad g'(L_t)\ge0.
\]

The paper does not address a discrete scheduled-fleet response. The atomic gap is to couple an upward-sloping learned price response with an exact or certifiable EVSP best-response oracle. The economic objective must be stated precisely:

- price-taking fleet: optimize at posted \(p_t\);
- strategic fleet bill: minimize \(L_tg_t(L_t)\), with marginal signal \(g_t(L_t)+L_tg'_t(L_t)\);
- social/dictator objective: minimize incremental generation cost \(\int_{U_t}^{U_t+L_t}g_t(q)dq\), not the fleet payment.

## 2. Ding, Li, and Jian (2022): shared-EV platform and DNO games

**Citation.** Yanyan Ding, Sen Li, and Sisi Jian, “Optimal pricing and fleet management for shared electric vehicle in coupled power and transport networks,” *Transportation Research Part C* 141 (2022), 103727.

### Actual application and method

The model couples a monopolistic ride-sourcing platform and a distribution-network operator through shared EV drivers. Drivers choose passenger transport service, V2G energy service, relocation, or idling. Travelers choose the platform or an outside option. Logit choice models capture traveler and driver behavior; Cobb–Douglas matching friction generates rider waiting and driver searching time; multi-period energy conservation tracks battery use. See Section 3, pp. 4–9.

The platform sets the passenger fare and driver commission to maximize profit. The DNO uses a simplified DistFlow/LPAC representation and sets discharge rewards while minimizing generation, flow, and procurement cost. Two institutional regimes are studied:

- simultaneous Nash competition between the DNO and platform; and
- DNO-leader Stackelberg competition in which the platform responds as follower.

The authors prove existence of the bilateral traveler–driver supply-demand equilibrium under their matching assumptions using Brouwer’s theorem. They do not analytically prove existence or uniqueness of the full Nash equilibrium. Dual decomposition, best response, enumeration, and a trust-region dogleg routine are combined to compute candidates; Nash and Stackelberg algorithms appear in Section 5, pp. 12–15.

### Numerical setting and results

The application uses August 2020 Hong Kong transport and power data but aggregates the city into four zones. There are approximately 2,000 modeled EVs, 60 kWh batteries, 30 kWh available at the start of the study window, and three one-hour demand regimes between 16:00 and 19:00. Travel demand is calibrated from taxi trip data and EV penetration. The power model focuses on varied peak load rather than the full 7,300 MWh baseline. See Section 6.1, pp. 16–18.

Numerical evidence includes:

- the nonlinear matching subproblem converging to a common solution from 1,000 random initializations, which is numerical evidence rather than a uniqueness theorem;
- low demand: 580 Nash or 592 Stackelberg EVs discharge; platform profit rises 3.64% or 1.58% relative to the no-energy-service baseline;
- medium demand: only 12 or 19 EVs discharge and the benefit nearly vanishes;
- high demand: no EVs discharge because passenger-service payoffs dominate;
- DNO leadership lowers the DNO’s peak operating cost but reduces platform and driver payoffs relative to Nash; and
- V2G reduces cruising at low demand, increases it at medium demand through price competition, and has no effect at high demand.

The four-zone Nash model takes about 30 minutes and Stackelberg about 45 minutes. A six-zone version takes approximately 60 and 90 minutes. The authors identify zone enumeration as the scaling bottleneck. See Sections 6.2–6.5 and Section 7, pp. 18–25.

### Assumptions and limitations

- This is ride-hailing with elastic passenger demand and optional energy service, not fixed scheduled public transport.
- The city is represented by only four zones; route and vehicle-duty detail is suppressed.
- Drivers and travelers follow calibrated-form logit models; the full equilibrium is nonlinear and nonconvex.
- Full Nash existence and uniqueness are not proved; uniqueness is tested numerically.
- The power network is simplified and the focus is V2G discharge during a three-hour peak window.
- Scaling the number of zones is subject to enumeration’s curse of dimensionality.
- Heterogeneous fleets, multiple competing platforms, heterogeneous passengers, heterogeneous charging rates, and renewable uncertainty are left for future work.

### Novelty killed

Generic claims of a first game-theoretic coupled transport-power pricing model, a first Nash/Stackelberg DNO–mobility-platform incentive model, or a first treatment of mobile EVs as temporal “storage” and spatial “wires” are unavailable.

### Atomic EVSP/learning/economic gap

The remaining distinction is a mandatory-service fleet whose private feasible set is a difficult EVSP. A useful game would have a market or DNO design a tariff, payment, or flexibility contract while anticipating an exact bus-duty and charging response. This changes the follower from a smooth elastic flow/logit system to a discontinuous integer schedule. Incentive compatibility, private route feasibility, minimum service obligations, and scalable equilibrium computation remain open.

## 3. Subramanian et al. (2022): bilevel conic fleet routing and endogenous LMPs

**Citation.** Vignesh Subramanian, Felipe Feijoo, Sriram Sankaranarayanan, Kevin Melendez, and Tapas K. Das, “A bilevel conic optimization model for routing and charging of EV fleets serving long distance delivery networks,” *Energy* 251 (2022), 123808.

### Actual application and method

The upper level chooses long-distance semi-truck routes and charging schedules using a weighted combination of charging cost and travel time. The route and charging decisions produce power loads. The lower level solves an AC optimal power flow with SOCP relaxation and returns locational marginal prices, so route-induced load changes price and price changes routes. KKT conditions convert the bilevel model into a single-level mixed-integer conic mathematical program with equilibrium constraints; bilinear binary–dual terms are handled with big-\(M\) linearization. See Section 4, pp. 4–7.

This is a centralized fleet leader anticipating the grid’s optimal dispatch response, not a Nash game among fleets. Charging-station locations are fixed. The upper objective traces a Pareto frontier between travel time and charging expenditure.

### Numerical setting and results

The case combines:

- the 24-node Sioux Falls road network;
- a modified PJM five-bus network with AC network constraints and five LMPs;
- seven charging stations;
- 50 OD pairs, each served by a fleet of 100 identical electric semi-trucks;
- a 12-hour 07:00–19:00 horizon;
- 500 kWh batteries, 20 kWh reserve, and 30 kWh desired arrival energy; and
- a maximum eight-hour solve time in Julia/Gurobi.

Nine preference weights are tested. Seven solve below 0.01% optimality gap; the \(r=0.7\) and \(r=0.8\) cases stop at 6% and 9% gaps after eight hours. Minimum travel time costs 17% more in charging expenditure than the most charging-cost-oriented solutions. When charging cost is emphasized, vehicles accept longer routes and later charging at lower-load buses. Average LMP falls from about 23.7 USD/MWh in the travel-time-oriented case to 22.3 USD/MWh in the charging-cost-oriented case. See Section 5, pp. 7–10.

### Assumptions and limitations

- Each OD cohort contains 100 identical vehicles and is represented as a route-choice fleet, not as individual vehicle duties covering mandatory trips.
- Adjacent-node travel times are simplified to one hour.
- Charging stations and the physical grid model are known; there is no learning from limited market data.
- The formulation is deterministic and centralized.
- There is no price-setting game, multiple fleet operators, or demand uncertainty.
- The conic MPEC scales poorly: even the stylized case reaches 6–9% gaps after eight hours for two settings.
- Large-network performance is not tested.

### Novelty killed

The paper directly kills a broad “first chicken-and-egg model where commercial-fleet routes determine charging load and nodal prices while prices determine routes” claim. It also kills an unqualified claim of a first bilevel routing-and-charging model with endogenous LMPs and ACOPF.

### Atomic EVSP/learning/economic gap

The missing unit is vehicle scheduling rather than OD path selection. A defensible contribution must incorporate fixed passenger-service trips, duty compatibility, fleet minimization, time-window and SOC feasibility, and charger choices in the lower response. The scalable method can exploit EVSP column generation rather than one monolithic MPEC. A second distinction is replacing a fully known grid with a learned or partially observed market response and comparing private strategic payment with system cost.

## 4. Chen et al. (2026): graph-attentive surrogate of price-responsive charging flow

**Citation.** Sheng Chen, Han Zhang, Qiuwei Wu, Peiyue Li, and Zhinong Wei, “Learning the Price Response of Spatiotemporal EV Charging Flow: A Graph-Attentive Surrogate Model,” *IEEE Transactions on Transportation Electrification* 12(2) (2026), 3767–3779. DOI: 10.1109/TTE.2026.3657374.

### Actual application and labels

A centralized multitemporal optimal traffic-power-flow NLP minimizes power procurement/generation cost plus travel-time and charging cost. EV users follow a Wardrop-type traffic assignment and choose paths in response to travel time and charging service fees (CSFs). Each selected EV path contains one fast charging station. The power model uses a radial DistFlow/SOCP formulation. See Section II, pp. 3–5.

The NLP is repeatedly solved offline under varied OD demand, link free-flow time, and CSFs. Its optimal charging-station flows become supervised labels. Thus the network learns a surrogate of an optimizer-generated response, not a causal response inferred from randomized market interventions.

### Surrogate architecture

Inputs include road-link capacity, travel time, charging service fee, OD demand, and topology. Links are treated as graph nodes so charging and bypass links can be represented explicitly. The model contains:

1. a two-layer GCN for local link-topology features;
2. bidirectional cross-attention between global OD demand and graph-node features; and
3. a transformer encoder with learnable positional encoding for multi-period dependencies.

A fully connected decoder outputs charging-station traffic flow over multiple periods. It does not output full routes, vehicle duties, SOC trajectories, or a feasible schedule. MSE is the training loss and Adam is the optimizer. See Section III, pp. 5–7.

### Training and numerical evidence

The synthetic case uses a modified 13-link Nguyen–Dupuis network, a modified IEEE 33-bus network, and five FCSs connected to buses 2, 10, 18, 25, and 28. The authors generate 14,000 five-period sample groups by varying OD demands and free-flow link times, then use a random 80/20 split. The model uses two GCN layers, hidden dimension 16, transformer embedding dimension 128, four attention heads, three encoder layers, dropout 0.1, batch size 32, and learning rate \(10^{-3}\). See Section IV and Tables II–III, pp. 7–8.

Test-set performance in Table IV, p. 9, is:

| Model | RMSE | MAE | \(R^2\) | Average error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| BP | 10.28 | 6.72 | 0.9817 | 5.27% |
| GRU | 10.91 | 6.71 | 0.9792 | 5.56% |
| Transformer | 8.55 | 5.16 | 0.9874 | 3.98% |
| GCN | 7.118 | 3.65 | 0.9913 | 2.84% |
| Proposed | 6.70 | 2.92 | 0.9922 | 2.26% |

IPOPT requires 73.8 seconds per case while the surrogate requires 35 milliseconds, a reported 2,108× speedup. A separate five-station Nanjing dataset gives \(R^2=0.9411\) and RMSE 749.28 kW over 144 hours. Dynamic CSF scheduling reduces modeled total social cost 3.5%, PDN operation cost 4.1%, PDN loss 4.7%, and generalized transport cost 3.3%. See Tables V–VI and Figures 8–14, pp. 9–11.

### Assumptions and limitations

- All EVs have the same initial SOC and charging power.
- Every selected path contains one FCS; full trip chains and duty schedules are absent.
- Topologies are static.
- Synthetic labels inherit all assumptions and potential errors of the centralized NLP.
- Random train/test splitting can overstate generalization across time, demand regimes, or networks.
- The Nanjing experiment demonstrates load tracking; as presented, it does not validate counterfactual response to an imposed price change.
- The network predicts aggregate station flow and is not feasibility preserving.
- No uncertainty intervals, OOD detection, active learning, or exact correction are part of the method.

### Novelty killed

A generic “learn nonlinear price-to-spatiotemporal charging response with a GNN/transformer” contribution is already published. So is the claim that a neural surrogate can replace slow coupled traffic-power optimization at millisecond speed.

### Atomic EVSP/learning/economic gap

The remaining learning problem is the response of a finite scheduled fleet:

\[
F_\theta(p,z,\text{instance})
\rightarrow
(\text{route cover},\text{charging realization},\text{fleet size},\text{hourly load},\text{value}).
\]

A practical first target is aggregate load and optimal value, followed by column or route-ranking assistance rather than unconstrained direct schedule generation. Thesis value comes from active sampling near equilibrium, uncertainty/OOD detection, cross-instance generalization, and exact EVSP correction. Joint learning of market response \(g_\phi(L,z)\) and fleet response \(F_\theta(p,z)\), with optimization-based feasibility and final certificates, remains distinct.

## 5. Wei et al. (2018): static coupled-network equilibrium and best response

**Citation.** Wei Wei, Lei Wu, Jianhui Wang, and Shengwei Mei, “Network Equilibrium of Coupled Transportation and Power Distribution Systems,” *IEEE Transactions on Smart Grid* 9(6) (2018), 6764–6779. DOI: 10.1109/TSG.2017.2723016.

### Actual transport and power models

The transportation side is a multi-community Wardrop user equilibrium with gasoline and electric vehicle flows. Fast charging stations are represented through extended graph modules with charging and bypass links. EV travel expense includes road travel time, charging time, queueing, and electricity cost. All EVs in a class receive the same fixed energy amount. A strictly convex traffic-assignment problem produces a unique link-flow user equilibrium, and adaptive path generation avoids enumerating every path. See Section II, pp. 4–9.

The power side is a radial branch-flow ACOPF relaxed to SOCP. Under stated no-reverse-flow and feasibility conditions, the relaxation is exact. FCS load enters the OPF and LMP dual variables return to the traffic assignment. See Section III, pp. 9–11.

### Fixed-point formulation and convergence logic

The network equilibrium is

\[
x=S_{NTE}(x),
\]

where the traffic user equilibrium is the best response to LMPs and the OPF/LMP solution is the best response to FCS charging load. Under continuity and feasibility assumptions, Brouwer’s theorem establishes existence. Local stability depends on the product of:

- the sensitivity of LMP to charging load;
- the sensitivity of charging-link flow to LMP; and
- per-EV energy demand.

The paper notes that flatter LMP curves and smaller energy demand aid stability. Uniqueness is discussed through restrictive contraction-like conditions but is not generally guaranteed because LMPs may be discontinuous. See Section IV, pp. 11–13.

Algorithm 2 alternates:

1. solve traffic assignment at fixed LMP;
2. convert charging-link flows to FCS power demand;
3. solve SOCP OPF and retrieve new LMPs; and
4. stop when combined flow and price changes fall below tolerance.

The authors explicitly warn that poor initialization or an unstable equilibrium can prevent convergence and suggest restricting changes between iterations. This is an established version of the price–route–load fixed-point loop.

### Numerical setting and results

The illustrative case uses a stylized ring expressway, a radial PDN, four road classes, and eight FCSs. Each charging visit consumes 50 kWh; charging-link capacity is 15 in a 100-vehicles/hour traffic base. TAP is solved with BARON and SOCP OPF with MOSEK. Starting from zero-charge LMPs, the method reaches its tolerance in four iterations. Higher per-EV energy increases LMP dispersion and the number of iterations, consistent with stronger coupling. See Section V, pp. 13–15.

### Assumptions and limitations

- Vehicles form nonatomic aggregate flows; no individual fleet has market power.
- The equilibrium is static rather than a timetable-based dynamic schedule.
- OD demands are fixed and each EV has an identical fixed charging request.
- The power model and all network parameters are known.
- LMP continuity, OPF feasibility, and local stability are assumptions or sufficient conditions, not universal properties.
- The test is stylized and demonstrates only four-iteration convergence in one regime.
- No learning, strategic fleet objective, service commitment, or discrete route cover appears.

### Novelty killed

The broad iteration

\[
p\rightarrow\text{routes/load}\rightarrow\text{OPF}\rightarrow p
\]

was formalized as a fixed point by 2018, including path generation, existence conditions, stability sensitivity, failure modes, and restricted-step remedies. An unqualified “first fixed-point price–route interaction” claim is not viable.

### Atomic EVSP/learning/economic gap

The missing case is an atomic scheduled fleet with a discontinuous integer response. EVSP route covers can jump when prices cross small thresholds, producing cycles, multiple equilibria, and fleet-size discontinuities that nonatomic Wardrop theory smooths away. A valuable contribution is a two-fidelity fixed-point method using re-realization for cheap iterations and exact column pricing for correction, together with convergence diagnostics and certificates on the discretized route space.

## 6. Song et al. (2025): dynamic coupled equilibrium with finite FCS queues and spillback

**Citation.** Yingjie Song, Dong Ngoduy, Takao Dantsuji, and Chuan Ding, “Dynamic equilibrium of the coupled transportation and power networks considering electric vehicles charging behavior,” *Transportation Research Part A* 199 (2025), 104590.

### Actual dynamic model

The transportation system is a stochastic dynamic user equilibrium for mixed EV and gasoline-vehicle traffic. Travelers choose departure time and path under perceived-cost errors. EV cost includes travel time, early/late arrival penalties, charging cost, detour, and FCS queue time. Feasible EV paths must include a charging station. A discrete multiclass propagation model enforces link sending and receiving capacity, node transfers, finite numbers of chargers, finite FCS waiting areas, and spillback from charging queues into upstream roads. See Sections 3–4, pp. 4–10.

The PDN uses a dynamic radial ACOPF/SOCP model. Charging load affects power flow and LMPs; LMPs affect perceived travel cost, departure time, path, and FCS choice. See Section 5, pp. 10–11.

### Improved fixed-point algorithm

The dynamic equilibrium satisfies

\[
\lambda^*=\mu(g^*(h^*(\lambda^*))),
\]

where \(h\) is the stochastic dynamic traffic pattern and \(\lambda\) is the LMP trajectory. Each iteration:

1. dynamically loads mixed traffic and computes path costs and charging load;
2. performs stochastic dynamic traffic assignment to obtain auxiliary path flows;
3. solves the dual ACOPF problem for LMPs;
4. updates path flow with an adaptive averaging step \(h^{k+1}=h^k+(y^k-h^k)/\beta_k\);
5. replaces the LMP with the latest OPF value; and
6. tests relative changes in both path flow and prices.

The adaptive \(\beta_k\) increases faster when residuals worsen and more slowly when they improve. The authors explicitly state that the endogenous dynamic cost mapping is not monotone, so strong convergence is not guaranteed; the procedure is heuristic and supported by empirical convergence. See Section 6, pp. 11–12.

### Numerical setting and results

The case combines a Nguyen network and IEEE 33-bus radial PDN:

- 21 transport nodes, 31 links, and 32 enumerated paths;
- four charging links and four FCSs with 30, 35, 35, and 40 plugs;
- waiting-area capacity of 20 EVs at each FCS;
- four-hour 16:00–20:00 window with 2.5-minute time steps;
- homogeneous 150 kW charging and ten-minute charging duration;
- charging efficiency 92% and 170 km added range; and
- electricity-price inputs of 157 USD/MWh before 18:00 and 171 USD/MWh afterward.

The proposed FP-S algorithm takes 42 seconds and eight iterations, versus 54 seconds/ten iterations and 63 seconds/twelve iterations for two comparators. See Section 7.2 and Table 3, pp. 12–13.

Congestion on several charging approaches falls by 71–89%. As EV demand grows, FCS utilization saturates around 30% additional demand; above that point, charging load and PDN cost plateau but queues spill back and transportation cost rises sharply. For OD pair (1,2), modeled EV path cost rises from 9.84 USD to 208.06 USD at 40% demand growth. See Sections 7.3–7.4, pp. 15–19, and Tables 4–5.

### Assumptions and limitations

- The model remains a nonatomic traveler equilibrium, not a fleet vehicle-scheduling problem.
- OD demand is fixed and all modeled charging EVs use homogeneous 150 kW, ten-minute service.
- Feasible paths are enumerated in advance for a small network.
- Convergence is empirical because monotonicity is absent.
- There is no strategic fleet operator, price-setting game, learned price response, or market-power objective.
- No empirical behavioral estimation validates how real travelers trade electricity price against queueing and travel time; the authors explicitly call for such studies.
- Home charging versus public fast charging is not differentiated.

### Novelty killed

Generic claims of a first dynamic transport-power price fixed point, first equilibrium with finite charger queues, or first treatment of queue spillback and real-time LMP feedback are unavailable.

### Atomic EVSP/learning/economic gap

The missing dynamic object is a scheduled fleet with mandatory service, not travelers selecting departure times. Charger capacity remains highly relevant, but the choice response must respect vehicle duties, timetable connections, SOC, terminal-energy policy, and fleet size. Strategic tariff or incentive design, learned response, scalable column generation, and convergence/certification for this discrete response remain open.

## 8. Implications for EVSP-DR experiments

### 8.1 Start with a controlled price-response model

Before claiming empirical market impact, use monotone synthetic load-side curves

\[
p_t(L_t)=a_t+b_tL_t,
\qquad b_t\ge0,
\]

so the true equilibrium and welfare integral are known. Sweep fleet scaling, market depth, damping, trust radius, and initial prices. Compare:

1. exogenous price-taking schedule;
2. undamped fixed-point best response;
3. damped or trust-region fixed point;
4. strategic bill minimization; and
5. integrated-generation-cost dictator optimization.

Record route-set hashes, not only load and price residuals, to distinguish a stable schedule from a numerical average over a discrete two-cycle.

### 8.2 Use a two-fidelity fleet oracle

Use fixed-trip-sequence charging re-realization for cheap inner iterations. Invoke exact expanded-network pricing when the route hash changes, a cycle is detected, reduced-cost evidence deteriorates, or every fixed number of iterations. Finish headline cases with an exact-pricing audit on the discretized route space.

An important implementation caveat is that trip-sequence feasibility may be price independent while the stored charging realization is not. Repricing an old charged route does not recover its best charging realization under a new tariff. Any price-parametric column method must either:

- re-realize the trip sequence under each new price;
- retain multiple nondominated charging realizations for the same trip set;
- represent charging cost parametrically; or
- rerun exact pricing.

### 8.3 Learn the two responses separately before coupling them

Grid response dataset:

`market, timestamp, product, base_load_mw, fleet_net_load_mw, clearing_price_per_mwh, context, train_cutoff`

Fleet response dataset:

`instance, price_id, iteration, station, hour, charge_kwh, load_mw, route_hash, fleet, electricity_cost, deadhead, charge_visits, initial_soc, terminal_soc, concurrency, oracle_tier, certificate_status`

Train only on dates or scenarios preceding each test case. Use blocked temporal and instance-level splits rather than random row splits. Start by predicting aggregate load and optimal value. Route or column ranking can be learned later, with all schedules revalidated by the exact model.

### 8.4 Keep economic regimes separate

The same converged load can have different interpretations depending on who controls prices and which transfer payments count as social costs. Every experiment should label one of:

- **price taker:** fleet treats prices as fixed;
- **strategic fleet buyer:** fleet internalizes how its load changes its bill;
- **market/DNO leader:** price or tariff is chosen anticipating the EVSP response;
- **social planner/dictator:** physical generation cost, service cost, and congestion are co-optimized; or
- **multi-fleet game:** multiple scheduled operators interact through common prices and charger/grid constraints.

Transfers such as tariff payments belong in private objectives but generally cancel in total welfare unless they create distributional or budget constraints.

## 9. Recommended claim discipline

Do not claim:

- that price–route iteration itself is new;
- that bilevel endogenous-price routing itself is new;
- that game-theoretic EV fleet incentives themselves are new;
- that charger-queue-coupled dynamic equilibrium itself is new; or
- that a graph neural price-response surrogate itself is new.

A defensible central claim would instead be:

> We study endogenous electricity-price feedback for a finite scheduled electric-vehicle fleet whose response is a combinatorial vehicle-scheduling problem. We develop a two-fidelity equilibrium method that couples learned or structural price response with EVSP column generation, uses learning to amortize repeated best responses, and restores feasibility and optimality evidence through exact correction. This exposes discrete cycling, multiplicity, strategic-payment, and welfare phenomena that are absent from nonatomic traffic-flow and continuous charging models.

That claim still needs verification against the broader EVSP-specific and algorithmic literature, but it survives the six close papers reviewed here.
