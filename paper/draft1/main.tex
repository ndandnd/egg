\title{Duty Assignment Before Grid Pricing in Electric-Bus Fleets:\\
An Exact Projection, Support Bounds and Their Limits}
\author{Nathan Cho}
\date{Research draft, revised 3 November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Can a bus operator commit to duties before observing grid prices? For two
common charging windows, we prove that one price-independent matching per
fleet size preserves the entire attainable load--cost set, including under
common conversion losses and vehicle-to-grid exports. The grid then selects
fleet size and charging, while alternative pairings of that size add no
flexibility. In a charge-only extension with consecutive convex load
thresholds, the physical-versus-convexified cost gap is strictly less than
one bus-day fee, even with hard grid limits and local backup. We characterize
exact support-failure intervals in a lossless subclass and distinguish them
from cycling price updates. On public German peak-run data at 400\,kWh,
support fails in 7 of 2,664 sampled scenarios while undamped prices cycle
in 397. On a German distribution-feeder study, physical minimum-bus duties
match numerical conic lower estimates within one cent in 66 of 72 primary
assignments; four are solver-infeasible and two retain wider bounds.
Binding voltage limits require local supply at the largest far depot, but
we find no additional capacity-charge saving from pairing. The reduction's
boundary matters: in 64 general hourly scheduling instances, a zero-price
duty commitment remains measurably suboptimal in 48 after a price-blind
minimum-energy tie rule. These results identify when duty choice can precede
pricing and where that simplification needs a separate certificate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A bus operator can often solve duty assignment before optimizing charging.
The computational attraction is clear: retain the chosen services on each
bus and adjust charging as electricity prices change. The unresolved question
is what this commitment loses. A cheap duty may force energy into an expensive
or grid-constrained window, and another assignment with exactly the same
number of buses may offer a different charging set. A valid separation
therefore needs a physical dominance argument, or a measured and bounded
commitment penalty.

We give such an argument for two synchronized service batches. For each
number of paired services, a matching minimizing mandatory early charging
contains the load--cost set of every other matching at that count. It can be
chosen before observing electricity prices or specifying the feeder cost.
This is one representative per fleet size; prices and network feasibility
may still determine which size to use. The distinction is essential outside
the class, where hourly charging and transitions can change the ranking of
committed duties.

Marginal-price support supplies a second diagnostic. Whole fleets create a
nonconvex load--cost set: a charging profile can be attainable only with a
more expensive fleet than a convexified planner assigns to it. This connects
assignment to the established nonconvex-pricing problem in electricity
markets \citep{oneill2005,gribikHoganPope2007,huaBaldick2017,schiro2016}.
Fixed-population charging aggregates, however detailed
\citep{mukhiAbate2023exact,mukhiLohoAbate2025exact,muller2019aggregation},
do not retain a choice of the population of vehicle duties.

This paper gives an exact account of that trade-off for a class that is simple
enough to solve and recognizable enough to matter: buses serve a morning peak
batch and an afternoon peak batch, may charge at the depot in a common midday
window, and must be full again after an overnight window. Any bus may serve at
most one morning and one afternoon run, and a declared compatibility graph
lists the allowed pairings.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Exact projection (\cref{thm:projection}).} For each matching
cardinality $m$, all assignments with $m$ pairs share every aggregate quantity
except one: the total early energy their paired buses must receive. A
matching minimizing that requirement contains the \emph{entire} physical
load--cost polygon of every other matching of the same size, at identical loads
and fees. The complete projection is therefore a union of at most $\nu+1$
polygons with at most five vertices each, with common charging and discharging
losses, a shared bidirectional connector and full overnight replenishment. Each
point has a finite, serial, state-of-charge-feasible realization.
\item \textbf{Anatomy of the support gap (\cref{prop:anatomy}).} In the
lossless case the gap $\Delta=D-\CH$ between physical and convexified planning
values is positive only if the marginal-cost spread between the two charging
windows reaches $f/\eta$, where $f$ is the daily vehicle cost and $\eta$ an
increment between consecutive forced-charging thresholds. The gap is at most
$\kappa\eta^2/8$, where $\kappa$ bounds the slope of that spread.
For an additive shift in the spread, \cref{prop:bands} gives the exact
failure intervals, including their endpoints' support status. Their total
width depends only on the variation in marginal spread between the first
and last forced-charging thresholds.
\item \textbf{Price dynamics (\cref{thm:iteration}).} For the damped
price-adjustment loop that posts the marginal cost of the last response, undamped
prices are eventually periodic with period at most two and can cycle even when a
supporting price exists. Sufficiently small constant steps converge at a
strict vertex. At a face-interior optimum, prices become trapped in an $O(a)$
band. Convergence there requires an exact tie and eventual selection of the
optimal physical endpoint. Diminishing steps recover the hull spread. With
quadratic costs the step-weighted load and vehicle fee also converge to the
hull solution.
\item \textbf{Where the reduction stops (\cref{sec:boundaries}).} A common
transition energy per pair preserves the reduction. With pair-specific
transition energy, replacement holds within each group having the same
cardinality and total transition draw. For a complete, physically feasible
lossless charge-only perfect layer we characterize exactly when a single
matching suffices. A separate complete-graph construction gives a positive
support gap with curvature in the midday load. With a
third service batch, fleet feasibility alone is strongly NP-complete. On one
public 37-service timetable outside the two-batch class, rational certificates
establish a strictly positive gap.
\item \textbf{Calibrated evidence (\cref{sec:study}).} Using the public
timetables of 18 German operators \citep{sistig2025dataset,sistig2025electrification},
we build morning and afternoon peak runs and compute when the obstruction can
arise. With 400\,kWh batteries, 15 of 18 operators admit a maximum-size
pairing with zero mandatory midday charge, giving a flat cost frontier.
Among the remaining three, the smallest critical marginal spread is
0.53\,EUR/kWh at a 150\,EUR/day vehicle cost. When failure
occurs, the gap is below 0.06\% of total daily cost. Undamped price iteration
nevertheless cycles in 15\% of scenarios, nearly all of which have no gap.
A German feeder comparison resolves numerical duty-first cost brackets
within one cent in 66/72 primary assignments (\cref{sec:feeder}).
The separate 64-instance hourly test in \cref{sec:boundaries} records the
limits of zero-price commitment, including an energy-aware tie control.
\end{enumerate}

The support criterion itself, matching integrality and convex-hull pricing are
classical tools, and we use them as such. The new content is the exact physical
description of the assignment-dependent load--cost set, together with the
consequences it makes explicit for support and price iteration. The model is
deliberately stylized: it abstracts from travel between services, staggered
availability, battery degradation and switching time. \Cref{sec:boundaries}
shows which of these abstractions are essential.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Electric-bus scheduling with charging.}
Exact and heuristic methods for the electric vehicle scheduling problem combine
service covering with charging decisions under station capacity
\citep{devos2024capacity}, scale through column generation
\citep{parmentier2023fleets}, or integrate flow and set-partitioning models
\citep{borndorfer2024integrated}. \citet{wu2022grid} add power-grid
characteristics and endogenous vehicle-to-trip assignment with overnight
charging. \citet[Sec.~4.2]{nath2024joint} jointly choose charging locations,
trip assignments and charging schedules under energy and contracted-power
charges. At each fleet size, \citet[Sec.~4.2]{dabcevic2026pareto} vary objective
weights to trace the trade-off between deadheading and charging cost at given
tariffs. Our question concerns the joint set of loads and fleet costs over
\emph{all} assignments, from which one can test support across tariffs.

\paragraph{Buses, tariffs and markets.}
\citet{wu2019gridinteractive} embed bus operations in a bi-level model with
distribution locational marginal prices. Their upper level is a tactical
frequency-setting problem with terminal opportunity charging; the lower level
clears a DC power-flow market, and the bi-level problem is solved through its
complementarity reformulation. Vehicle-to-service assignment is not modeled, so
the assignment nonconvexity studied here does not arise. \citet{zoltowskaLin2021}
aggregate bus charging into day-ahead auction bids;
\citet{blatiak2022v2g} optimize commercial-fleet trip timing and charging
against energy and balancing prices; and \citet{zhou2025v2g} formulate a
multi-depot mixed-integer charging and discharging schedule for electric buses
under vehicle-to-grid tariffs and solve it with an adaptive large-neighbourhood
search (our comparison with the last is limited to its published abstract). All of
these studies address operational scheduling or market participation. Our
comparison concerns the proof that one assignment realizes every other
assignment's attainable loads at the same fleet size.

\paragraph{Aggregate flexibility.}
For a fixed EV population, aggregate flexibility is a Minkowski sum of
individual sets \citep{mukhiAbate2023exact,altaha2024flexibility}.
\citet[Thms.~3 and~7]{mukhiLohoAbate2025exact} characterize that sum and
disaggregate its schedules for generalized polymatroids.
\citet[Eqs.~(15)--(17)]{muller2019aggregation} also compute the least cost of
disaggregation for zonotopic resources with structured costs.
\citet{arvis2026aggregated} embed aggregate EV flexibility in unit commitment,
and \citet{altahaBitar2024lossy} give a state-of-charge reformulation for a
single lossy storage device. In our setting a matching determines which duties
enter the sum. At a fixed number of pairs the assignments have the same fleet
cost and aggregate battery bounds. Only their mandatory early purchases
differ, so one matching realizes the union of all their load polygons
(\cref{thm:projection,rem:netinterval}).

\paragraph{Nonconvex pricing.}
Uplift, convex-hull pricing and their computation are established
\citep{oneill2005,gribikHoganPope2007,huaBaldick2017,schiro2016,andrianesis2022hull}.
For linear market participants with a complete linear description,
\citet[Thm.~1]{huembs2022complete} characterize support by the existence
of an integral optimum of the relaxed planner's problem. \Cref{prop:support}
uses the corresponding convex-hull criterion for a complete fleet and a
convex supplier. Our gap bound uses the distance between adjacent fleet
choices. Shapley--Folkman bounds instead relate the effect of individual
nonconvexities to market aggregation \citep{starr1969,hreinsson2021}.

\paragraph{Price adjustment.}
\citet[Alg.~1, Eq.~(32), Cor.~4.1]{ma2016decentralized} update posted prices
toward the marginal generation cost of the latest EV response and prove
convergence under a contraction condition. We study this update with a
discontinuous response that selects physical layer endpoints. For an affine
marginal-cost spread $g$ and an initial price $\pi_0=g(q_0)$ at a feasible
early load $q_0$, the update is the load component of Frank--Wolfe on the hull
\citep[Alg.~1]{jaggi2013frankwolfe}. Its linear oracle minimizes
$c+g(q_k)E$, and $q_k=g^{-1}(\pi_k)$ satisfies
$q_{k+1}=(1-a_k)q_k+a_k E_k$. Ergodic recovery in convex dual methods is
also classical \citep{larsson1999ergodic,nedic2009approximate}.
\Cref{thm:iteration} identifies which fleet responses recur and how the
face they expose determines support. Within its constant-step trapping band,
the two endpoint branches, away from exact ties, rescale to the contracted
rotation studied by \citet{laurent2018rotations}.

\section{Complete fleets and the meaning of support}\label{sec:model}

Fix mandatory services, movement possibilities, a charging horizon divided into
market intervals $t=1,\dots,T$, and physical equipment. A \emph{complete plan}
$x\in\X$ covers every mandatory service exactly once, assigns services to used
buses, routes them, and schedules charging subject to battery and shared-resource
limits. Let $e(x)\in\R^T$ be the fleet's net grid energy by interval (negative
for exports) and $c(x)$ its intrinsic operating cost, here the vehicle cost.
The projected image $S=\{(e(x),c(x)):x\in\X\}$ is assumed nonempty and compact.

A supplier serves the fleet's incremental demand at a proper closed convex cost
$F:\R^T\to\R\cup\{+\infty\}$, finite on all attainable loads. The
\emph{physical} and \emph{convexified} planning values and the \emph{gap} are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DCH}
 D=\min_{x\in\X}\{c(x)+F(e(x))\},\qquad
 \CH=\min_{(e,c)\in\conv S}\{c+F(e)\},\qquad
 \Delta=D-\CH\ge0.
\end{equation}
The hull mixes complete plans. Its optimum is a schedule mixture, not one
executable day. At a posted price vector $p\in\R^T$ the fleet's response value
and the regret of plan $x$ are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regret}
 V(p)=\min_{y\in\X}\{c(y)+p^\top e(y)\},\qquad r(x;p)=c(x)+p^\top e(x)-V(p).
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Own-load support]\label{def:support}
A physical plan $x$ is \emph{supported} if some marginal price
$p\in\partial F(e(x))$ gives $r(x;p)=0$.
\end{definition}

The operator in \eqref{eq:regret} controls the complete fleet and takes the posted
price as fixed. This is the decentralization test behind marginal-cost tariffs:
a price computed from the operator's own load must not give it a reason to
deviate. A strategic operator anticipating the price change would solve a
different problem, which we do not study.

\begin{proposition}[Classical support criterion]\label{prop:support}
For every physical $x$ and $p\in\partial F(e(x))$, $r(x;p)\ge c(x)+F(e(x))-\CH\ge\Delta$.
If $\relint\conv e(\X)\cap\relint\operatorname{dom}F\neq\emptyset$, then some
physical plan is supported if and only if $\Delta=0$, and in that case every
physical optimum is supported by every maximizer of $V(p)-F^*(p)$.
\end{proposition}

This specializes standard arguments \citep{oneill2005,gribikHoganPope2007,huaBaldick2017};
the proof is in \cref{app:support}. Two consequences are used below. A positive
gap gives every physical plan a regret of at least $\Delta$ at its own marginal
prices. Conversely, at a maximizer $p^*$ of $V(p)-F^*(p)$ (a convex-hull price), the
identity in the proof shows that a physical optimum's regret is at most $\Delta$,
so a payment conditional on implementing that particular schedule can remove
the operator's incentive to deviate at the hull price. A strict gap still
excludes own-load support as defined above.

\section{An exact assignment-dependent projection}\label{sec:projection}

\paragraph{The two-batch class.}
There are $n_A\ge1$ morning (A) services during $[0,1]$ and $n_B\ge1$ afternoon
(B) services during $[2,3]$, with battery draws $0<a_i,b_j\le B$ for a common
usable capacity $B>0$. Same-batch services overlap, so each bus serves at most
one A and one B service. A bipartite graph $\Gamma$ lists the allowed A--B
pairings; every service may also be served by its own bus. A partial matching
$M\subseteq\Gamma$ with $|M|=m$ uses $N_m=n_A+n_B-m$ buses, each at cost $f>0$;
let $\nu$ be the maximum matching size. Transitions take no time or energy
(\cref{sec:boundaries} relaxes this). All buses start full, are at the depot
throughout the \emph{early} window $[1,2]$, and must be full again after the
\emph{terminal} window $[3,4]$. One shared early connector moves at most $K\ge0$
grid-side energy in either direction; one terminal connector delivers at most
$H\ge0$, charging only. Every bus accepts the full connector power throughout
each corresponding unit window. Service draw is nonnegative throughout a
run, and there is no auxiliary draw outside the specified tasks.
Charging and discharging efficiencies are common,
$\alpha,\delta\in(0,1]$. Early actions may change direction finitely often, are
sequential on the connector, and incur no switching delay or cycling cost.

Let $C$ and $X$ be the integrated early purchases and exports. The connector's
gross use is $C+X\le K$ and the net early load is $E=C-X$. Write
\[
 S_0=\sum_i a_i+\sum_j b_j,\qquad P=\sum_i a_i,\qquad Q=\alpha C-X/\delta.
\]
$Q$ is the fleet's net battery gain in the early window. Full replenishment then
requires the terminal purchase $L=(S_0-Q)/\alpha$. The cost $F$ is a function of
the two window loads $(E,L)$.

\begin{theorem}[Complete common-window projection]\label{thm:projection}
For $m=0,\dots,\nu$ let
\[
 r_m=\min_{\substack{M\text{ matching in }\Gamma\\ |M|=m}}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in M}(a_i+b_j-B)_+,
\]
\[
 W_m=\Bigl\{(C,X):\ C\ge r_m/\alpha,\ X\ge0,\ C+X\le K,\
 \max\{S_0-N_mB,\ S_0-\alpha H\}\le \alpha C-X/\delta\le P\Bigr\}.
\]
The set of attainable (early load, terminal load, vehicle cost) triples is exactly
\[
 \mathcal P=\bigcup_{m=0}^{\nu}\Bigl\{\bigl(C-X,\ (S_0-\alpha C+X/\delta)/\alpha,\ fN_m\bigr):(C,X)\in W_m\Bigr\}.
\]
Every point has a realization with finitely many serial actions. Each nonempty
$W_m$ is a polygon with at most five vertices, so $\conv\mathcal P$ is generated
by at most $5(\nu+1)$ points. If exports are prohibited, the same holds with $X=0$.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Why one matching statistic suffices.}
Fix a matching. A bus's net early battery gain $x_v$ lies in an interval:
$[a_i+b_j-B,\,a_i]$ for a paired bus, $[a_i-B,\,a_i]$ for an A-only bus and
$[b_j-B,\,0]$ for a B-only bus. The lower endpoints sum to $S_0-N_mB$ and the
upper endpoints to $P$, whatever the matching. Only the sum $R$ of the
\emph{positive} lower endpoints, the energy that paired buses must receive
before their afternoon service, depends on which services are paired. At any
admissible total $Q$, the intervals can be filled so that positive gains total
$\max\{R,Q\}$, giving one-direction purchases $\max\{R,Q\}/\alpha$ and
exports $\delta(\max\{R,Q\}-Q)$. Any additional purchase $z\ge0$ with matching
exports $\alpha\delta z$ is realized by battery-neutral charge--discharge cycles
on one bus, in chunks of depth at most $B/2$. Serializing all actions uses exactly
$C+X$ of connector capacity. At fixed $m$, lowering $R$ only relaxes the
constraint $C\ge R/\alpha$, so a minimum-requirement matching contains the polygon of every other
matching of that size. This is set equality, not just economic dominance; the
complete proof is in \cref{app:projection}.

\begin{remark}[What a net-interval aggregate loses]\label{rem:netinterval}
Two fleets whose battery-gain intervals sum to the same net interval can have
different mandatory purchases: $[1,2]$ and $[-1,0]$ sum to $[0,2]$, as do
$[0,1]$ and $[0,1]$, but the first pair forces at least one unit of purchase.
With losses and a gross connector budget this difference changes the attainable
$(E,L)$ set. The statistic $r_m$ is exactly the information about the
assignment that the aggregate must keep.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The lossless charge-only case.}
With $\alpha=\delta=1$ and no exports, each layer reduces to an interval of early
loads. Let $\mathrm{Lo}=\max\{0,S_0-H\}$, $U=\min\{P,K\}$ and
$\ell_m=\max\{\mathrm{Lo},r_m\}$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lossless}
 \mathcal P=\bigcup_{m:\ \ell_m\le U}\{(E,\,S_0-E,\,fN_m):\ \ell_m\le E\le U\},
\end{equation}
the model is feasible exactly when $\mathrm{Lo}\le U$, and $r_m$ is nondecreasing and
discretely convex in $m$ (\cref{app:projection}). Let $m_0$ be the largest $m$
with $r_m\le\mathrm{Lo}$ and $m_{\max}$ the largest $m$ with $\ell_m\le U$. The
lower boundary $\bar c(E)$ of the hull's operating cost is piecewise linear. Call
$\mathrm{Lo}$ and $r_m$ for $m_0<m\le m_{\max}$ the \emph{breakpoints}. The boundary
starts at $(\mathrm{Lo},fN_{m_0})$, interpolates linearly between consecutive
breakpoints $(\ell_{m-1},fN_{m-1})$ and $(\ell_m,fN_m)$, and is flat at
$fN_{m_{\max}}$ from the last breakpoint to $U$ (the \emph{flat face}).
Consecutive breakpoints are one cardinality apart, so each sloping segment has slope
$-f/\eta_m$ with increment $\eta_m=\ell_m-\ell_{m-1}>0$.

\begin{corollary}[Support classification]\label{cor:support}
Let $G(E)=F(E,S_0-E)$ be continuous and strictly convex on $[\mathrm{Lo},U]$ and let
$E^*$ minimize $\bar c+G$. Every early load in $[\mathrm{Lo},U]$ is attainable.
A physical plan attains the hull cost $\bar c(E^*)$ at that load if and only if
$E^*$ is a breakpoint or belongs to $[\ell_{m_{\max}},U]$. In those cases
$\Delta=0$. The gap is positive exactly when $E^*$ lies strictly between
consecutive breakpoints. Under the relative-interior condition of
\cref{prop:support}, the zero-gap cases are exactly those admitting
own-load support.
\end{corollary}

For a complete graph, $r_m$ has a closed form: sort both batches
increasingly and pair the $m$ smallest morning draws with the $m$ smallest
afternoon draws in opposite order,
$r_m=\sum_{i=1}^m(a_{(i)}+b_{(m+1-i)}-B)_+$ (a classical exchange argument,
\cref{app:projection}). For a general graph, $r_m$ is a
minimum-cost matching of cardinality $m$.

\paragraph{A worked example.}
Take morning draws $(5,15)$, afternoon draws $(14,15)$, capacity $B=20$, $K=15$,
$H=49$, $f=7$, and the complete graph. Then $(r_0,r_1,r_2)=(0,0,9)$: the two-bus crossed pairing needs
9\,kWh of midday charge, while pairing by index would need 10. With
$F(E,L)=4E+(E^2+L^2)/20$, the physical optimum is the two-bus plan at $E=9$,
with $D=2681/20$. The hull optimum lies at $E^*=151/18$ on the segment joining the
three-bus plan at $E=0$ to the two-bus plan at $E=9$, with $\CH=434201/3240$ and
$\Delta=121/3240$ (\cref{fig:anchor}a). At its own marginal prices the physical
optimum has regret $11/10$, about thirty times $\Delta$: the support failure
costs the operator far more in bill terms than the planning gap costs the system.

\section{Price formation on the exact class}\label{sec:prices}

We study the lossless class \eqref{eq:lossless}. With bidirectional exchange
the layers are $[t_m,U]$ with $t_m=\max\{S_0-H,\,S_0-N_mB,\,2r_m-K\}$.
The results below apply with $\mathrm{Lo}=t_0$, $\ell_m=t_m$,
$m_0=\max\{m:t_m=t_0\}$ and $m_{\max}=\max\{m:t_m\le U\}$
(\cref{app:projection}).

On the physical set, the terminal load is $S_0-E$. A posted price $p=(p_E,p_L)$
therefore charges $p_LS_0+\pi E$ with $\pi=p_E-p_L$, and the operator's response
depends only on the \emph{spread} $\pi$. Assume $\mathrm{Lo}<U$, and let $F$ be convex
and differentiable on a neighbourhood of the attainable loads. Let
$g(E)=\partial_EF-\partial_LF$ at $(E,S_0-E)$. This is the marginal-cost spread
at early load $E$, and it equals $G'(E)$. Assume $g$ is continuous and strictly
increasing on $[\mathrm{Lo},U]$, let $E^*$ be the unique hull optimum, and let
$\pi^*=g(E^*)$, the \emph{convex-hull spread}. We study a fleet solver that
returns a layer endpoint. Write $R(\pi)$ for the loads of all physical layer
endpoints minimizing $fN_m+\pi E$. This includes an endpoint at a collinear
breakpoint when several cardinalities tie. At $\pi=0$, interior loads of the
cheapest layer also minimize the bill and lie outside this selection rule.

\paragraph{Anatomy of the gap.}

\begin{proposition}[Gap conditions]\label{prop:anatomy}
Let $\eta_{\max}=\max_{m_0<m\le m_{\max}}\eta_m$ (zero if there is no sloping
face).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),itemsep=1pt]
\item If $\Delta>0$, then $E^*$ lies strictly between consecutive breakpoints
$u<u'$ with $\pi^*=f/(u'-u)$. In particular, $\eta_{\max}>0$ and
$\max_{[\mathrm{Lo},U]}g>f/\eta_{\max}$.
\item If $g$ is $\kappa$-Lipschitz, then $\Delta\le\kappa\eta_{\max}^2/8$.
When $\Delta>0$, the sharper bound is $\kappa(u'-u)^2/8$ for the interval in (i).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

For an optimum between consecutive breakpoints, the marginal spread times
the forced early energy equals the vehicle cost saved by one more pair.
Varying the spread intercept identifies the supply curves for which a mixture
improves on every physical schedule.

\begin{proposition}[Exact intercept bands]\label{prop:bands}
Fix the physical layers and $f>0$. Let $G_s'(E)=s+h(E)$ for $s\in\mathbb R$,
where $h$ is continuous and strictly increasing. The intercepts producing a
positive gap are precisely
\begin{equation}\label{eq:interceptbands}
 \{s:\Delta(s)>0\}=
 \bigcup_{m=m_0+1}^{m_{\max}}
 \left(\frac{f}{\eta_m}-h(\ell_m),\
       \frac{f}{\eta_m}-h(\ell_{m-1})\right).
\end{equation}
These open intervals are disjoint. Their total length on the real line is
$h(\ell_{m_{\max}})-h(\mathrm{Lo})$, independent of $f>0$. If there are no
sloping segments the union is empty and its length is zero.
\end{proposition}

For quadratic window costs with $h(E)=\beta(2E-S_0)$ and $\beta>0$, the total
length is $2\beta(\ell_{m_{\max}}-\mathrm{Lo})$. The fee sets the location
of each interval, so the length within a given tariff range and the number of
hits on a finite grid can depend on $f$.

\begin{proposition}[One-fee rounding bound]\label{prop:onefee}
In a charge-only two-window projection, suppose consecutive pair counts
$m=0,\ldots,\nu$ have early-energy intervals $[\ell_m,U]$ with a common
upper endpoint, nondecreasing discrete-convex $\ell_m$, and fee $f(N-m)$,
where $f>0$. Let $G(E)$ be any common grid and supply cost, possibly taking
value $+\infty$ to impose hard feasibility. If the convexified optimum is
finite and attained at mean count $t$ and energy $E$, then
\[
 0\le D-\CH\le f\bigl(t-\lfloor t\rfloor\bigr)<f.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\ell(t)$ interpolate consecutive lower endpoints. Discrete convexity
makes $\ell(t)$ convex. Jensen's inequality and mixing the neighboring
layers show that the hull at mean count $t$ has exactly the energy interval
$[\ell(t),U]$. Its fee is $f(N-t)$. The integer count $\lfloor t\rfloor$
admits the same $E$, because $\ell_{\lfloor t\rfloor}\le\ell(t)\le E\le U$.
Keep its entire grid dispatch and any backup supply. Only the fee changes,
by $f(t-\lfloor t\rfloor)$. A physical optimum can do no worse.
\end{proof}

The constant is sharp. Two services each draw one unit, the usable battery
holds one, and pairing forces one unit of early charge. Two singleton buses
admit $[0,1]$. With free supply below an early cap $1-\varepsilon$ and
infeasibility above, subtract the common one-bus fee. The physical cost is
$f$ and the hull cost $f\varepsilon$, approaching a gap of $f$.
The bound applies to the entire schedule, irrespective of the number of
binding network constraints. It does not extend by counting binding windows
in an arbitrary multidimensional scheduling problem.

A cap between forced levels is insufficient for a positive gap: the optimum
may occur elsewhere or at a supported tie. Price ambiguity is also
unnecessary. With fee one on $[0,1]$, fee zero at $E=1$, and smooth cost
$G(E)=E^2$, physical cost is one and hull cost is $3/4$, at the unique
marginal price one. Support failure and an update orbit need separate tests.

\paragraph{Price iteration.}
A natural way to find a supporting price is to post a price, observe the
fleet's response, and move the price toward the marginal cost of that response:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iteration}
 \pi_{k+1}=(1-a_k)\pi_k+a_k\,g(E_k),\qquad E_k\in R(\pi_k),\quad a_k\in(0,1].
\end{equation}
Applied componentwise to $(p_E,p_L)$, the damped update
$p_{k+1}=(1-a_k)p_k+a_k\nabla F(e_k)$ induces \eqref{eq:iteration} on the
spread. At a fixed spread $\pi=g(E)$ the selected plan is supported by its own
marginal-price vector. We call a corner or domain endpoint of the lower
frontier a \emph{strict vertex}. A maximal straight segment of positive length
is a \emph{face}. Let $\mathcal T$ consist of the negatives of the slopes of
these faces, the spreads at which their endpoints tie.

\begin{lemma}[Sign property]\label{lem:sign}
For every $\pi$ and every $E\in R(\pi)$, $(\pi-\pi^*)(g(E)-\pi^*)\le0$.
Consequently, for every step $a\in(0,1]$,
$|\pi_{k+1}-\pi^*|\le\max\{(1-a)|\pi_k-\pi^*|,\,aM\}$, where
$M=\max_{E\in[\mathrm{Lo},U]}|g(E)-\pi^*|$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Price iteration on the two-batch class]\label{thm:iteration}
Under the standing assumptions of this section, the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),itemsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Fixed points.} Some vertex selection has a fixed point if and only if
$E^*$ is a breakpoint or $E^*=U$. In particular there is no fixed point when $\Delta>0$, nor when $E^*$ lies
inside the flat face, although $\Delta=0$ in the latter case.
\item \emph{Undamped iteration} ($a_k\equiv1$, a deterministic selection). Every orbit is eventually
periodic with period one or two. If $E^*$ lies in the relative interior of a
maximal face $[w,w']$ and $\pi_0\neq\pi^*$, the period is two and the cycle's loads satisfy
$E_{\rm lo}\le w<w'\le E_{\rm hi}$.
\item \emph{Constant step} $a\in(0,1)$. Every orbit enters the interval
$|\pi-\pi^*|\le aM$ after finitely many steps and remains there. If $E^*$ is a strict
vertex and $aM<\operatorname{dist}(\pi^*,\mathcal T\setminus\{\pi^*\})$
(distance to the empty set is infinite), then $E_k=E^*$ eventually and
$\pi_k\to\pi^*$ geometrically. If $E^*$ lies inside a face $[w,w']$,
convergence requires an exact hit on $\pi^*$ followed by eventual selection
of $E^*$ at every step. This is possible for at most countably many initial
spreads and requires $E^*$ to be a breakpoint. Under the same step bound,
all sufficiently late responses lie on $[w,w']$ and
\[
 \pi_k\in[\pi^*-a\,d_-,\ \pi^*+a\,d_+],\qquad
 d_-=\pi^*-g(w),\quad d_+=g(w')-\pi^* .
\]
Within this band the response is $w$ above $\pi^*$ and $w'$ below $\pi^*$.
Both endpoints recur infinitely often if the orbit eventually avoids $\pi^*$
or if the tie rule at $\pi^*$ always chooses an endpoint of the maximal face.
\item \emph{Diminishing steps} ($a_k\to0$, $\sum_ka_k=\infty$). Then $\pi_k\to\pi^*$,
and the step-weighted averages of $g(E_k)$ converge to $\pi^*$. If $g$ is affine,
the step-weighted averages of load and vehicle cost converge to $E^*$ and
$\bar c(E^*)$, respectively.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\Cref{lem:sign} follows from monotonicity of the hull frontier's
subdifferential (\cref{app:prices}).
\Cref{fig:anchor}b illustrates all three regimes on the worked example. The
step-size conditions in (c) matter. In the worked example with the steeper supply
$F(E,L)=16E+(E^2+L^2)/4$, the hull optimum is the supported vertex $E^*=9$, yet
undamped iteration from $\pi_0=3$ alternates between loads $0$ and $15$ forever,
while the step $a=1/10$ converges. Part (b) also assumes a selection that depends
only on the current price; history-dependent tie rules are outside its scope.

\paragraph{Ties and support.} An optimum strictly between consecutive
breakpoints has a positive gap. A breakpoint in the interior of a longer
sloping face has zero gap and can be selected at the exact tie. An optimum
inside the flat face also has zero gap. The cheapest fleet can deliver it,
although the endpoint rule selects only the ends of that layer. These cases can
all exhibit persistent spread fluctuations. The empirical frequencies in
\cref{sec:study} refer to the stated tie rule and initial spread.

An exact tie can change which loads recur. Take three services in each batch,
all draws and $B$ equal to one, a complete graph, $K=3$, $H=6$ and $f=1$.
Here $\ell_m=m$ and $\bar c(E)=6-E$ on $[0,3]$. With
$F(E,L)=E^2/2+(4/3)L$, the optimal spread $\pi^*=1$ occurs at $E^*=7/3$.
At $\pi=1$ let the solver choose the physical endpoint $E=1$. Starting there
with $a=1/2$ gives the spread cycle $1,1/3,1$ and the repeating loads $1,3$.
The maximal face has left endpoint $0$, which this orbit never visits.

\paragraph{Averaging.} With an affine spread and diminishing steps, part (d)
recovers the hull load and vehicle cost using weights $a_k/\sum_{j<n}a_j$.
Convexity gives $G(\sum_k\lambda_kE_k)\le\sum_k\lambda_k G(E_k)$ for any
mixture weights $\lambda_k$. Every executed day costs at least $D$, even
when the weighted load and vehicle cost converge to a hull point of cost
$\CH<D$. A constant step gives the band in (c). Its daily loads can have a
mean different from $E^*$.

\section{Where the reduction stops}\label{sec:boundaries}

The reduction rests on one fact: at fixed cardinality, total energy and every
aggregate bound except the mandatory purchase are independent of the
matching. We now show which changes preserve that fact and which destroy it.

\paragraph{Common transition energy preserves it.} If every matched pair performs
the same compulsory task of energy $d\ge0$ during a noncharging interval
between the early window and the B batch, then total consumption is $S_0+md$, the
paired interval becomes $[a_i+b_j+d-B,a_i]$, and edges with $b_j+d>B$ are removed.
\Cref{thm:projection} holds with these substitutions
(\cref{app:boundaries}).

\begin{proposition}[Matching replacement with transition energy]\label{prop:transitionclasses}
In the same noncharging interval, let edge $(i,j)$ require a compulsory
draw $d_{ij}\ge0$. Discard edges with $b_j+d_{ij}>B$. Group matchings by
cardinality $m$ and transition total $z=\sum_{(i,j)\in M}d_{ij}$. In each
nonempty group a matching minimizing $R(M)=\sum_{(i,j)\in M}(a_i+b_j+d_{ij}-B)_+$
realizes the whole group's set of $(E,L,fN_m)$. Its polygon is given by
\cref{thm:projection} with $S_0+z$ in place of $S_0$ and this minimum in
place of $r_m$, including losses and exports. Thus an invariant transition
total suffices for one matching to represent a cardinality layer. In particular,
if $d_{ij}=u_i+v_j$ on the allowed edges, every perfect matching of two
equal batches has total $\sum_i u_i+\sum_j v_j$.

For the lossless charge-only perfect layer of a complete $n\times n$ graph
with $n\ge2$, suppose every perfect matching admits a physical plan. One
perfect matching realizes every attainable triple of that layer if and only
if all perfect matchings have the same transition total. This is equivalent
to $d_{ij}+d_{i'j'}=d_{ij'}+d_{i'j}$ on every $2\times2$ submatrix, or
equivalently to $d_{ij}=u_i+v_j$ for row and column potentials.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{When transition totals differ.}
Different totals can require different representative matchings.

\begin{proposition}[Support failure on a complete assignment graph]\label{prop:completegraph}
For every $k\ge1$ there is a charge-only two-batch instance with $2k$
services per batch, the complete assignment graph, pair-dependent compulsory
transition energies, and a fleet cap of $2k$, in which every perfect matching is
physically feasible and, for the strongly convex-in-$E$ supply
$F(E,L)=3E+L+(E-3)^2/(2K)$ and any $f\ge0$,
\[
 D=\CH+\frac{9}{2K}>\CH.
\]
\end{proposition}

The construction and proof are in \cref{app:boundaries}. Every assignment
alternative is admitted, so the gap is not an artifact of a sparse
compatibility graph or of infeasible pairings. The gap shrinks as $K$ grows; the
statement is an existence result, not a prevalence claim.

\paragraph{A third batch makes feasibility hard.}

\begin{proposition}[Three batches]\label{prop:threebatch}
With three successive batches of $n$ simultaneous services, complete zero-energy
compatibility between consecutive batches, no charging before the last batch,
fleet cap $n$ and an adequate terminal connector, deciding whether a feasible
fleet exists is strongly NP-complete.
\end{proposition}

The proof reduces from numerical three-dimensional matching
\citep[problem SP16]{garey1979} (\cref{app:boundaries}). It rules out a
general polynomial-time extension of the two-batch algorithm to this
three-batch feasibility problem unless P equals NP.

\paragraph{Positive setup time.} The construction in \cref{thm:projection} uses
arbitrarily many short neutral cycles and zero switching time. A positive setup
time per action breaks exactness: it can make a layer infeasible while
neighbouring layers stay feasible, and the number of buses needing early charge
then matters separately from $r_m$. The companion setup-time proof in the repository bounds the change in
the gap when every relevant layer has a feasible point with sufficient
unused connector time. Whether actual depot windows provide enough slack for
this bound to be informative remains open.

\paragraph{Beyond the class: one certified public timetable.}
The 37 services in the Hildenbrand timetable of \citet{sistig2025dataset} have
staggered start times. We use its directed deadhead matrix and model charging at
one depot with a shared 360\,kW connector, 400\,kWh batteries, a vehicle cost of
100 and a synthetic quadratic hourly supply cost. A mixture of two feasible
plans bounds $\CH$ from above. A rational branch proof and separate fleet-count
bounds cover every physical plan. Two energy models give the same outward bounds,
\[
 \CH\le 441.142238<441.532912\le D\le 441.532913,
 \qquad D-\CH\ge 0.390674.
\]
The certified separation is at least $0.08848\%$ of $D$. The hull optimum and
the exact gap remain unknown. By \cref{prop:support}, no physical plan is
supported at its own marginal prices. The first certificate uses modeled
consumption stored on a finite movement graph. The second uses exact additive
consumption and extends to finite walks over the specified location pairs
under the travel and depot-access assumptions in \cref{app:public}.

\paragraph{A duty-first boundary test on hourly schedules.}
We also use all 64 previously certified synthetic development instances
(16 seeds, 8 or 12 trips, and two affine supply slopes). These have
hourly depot charging, travel between services, 60\,kWh batteries and
terminal reserve rather than full replenishment. Costs are in SEK.
Choose operating-cost-minimum duties at zero electricity price, retain
their ordered trip partition, and reoptimize charging and direct/depot
transitions against the original system objective. Adaptive tangent MILPs
and the original joint certificates give a regret interval for every cell.
All 64 fixed-duty problems meet a 0.01\,SEK optimality tolerance. The
arbitrary zero-price tie gives a regret lower endpoint above 0.01 in
53 cases, mean regret interval $[12.192,12.207]$\,SEK, and maximum upper
endpoint 54.094\,SEK.

To test the role of that tie, a separate prespecified sensitivity chooses
minimum charging energy among the zero-price operating-cost optima, before
observing the time-dependent prices. It still loses more than 0.01\,SEK
in 48 of 64 cells. The mean regret interval is $[7.753,7.769]$\,SEK and
the largest upper endpoint 46.126\,SEK; the largest total-cost percentage
upper bound is 2.043\%. No interval contradicts the original joint
certificate. This marks a boundary of these duty-first rules. It does not
rule out a better price-independent rule or establish a population claim.
The historical joint bounds were reused, with the original instance and
market sources; neither the German feeder nor its tariffs were attached to
these synthetic cases.

\section{Tariff sensitivity on public peak-run data}\label{sec:study}

The theory says when support can fail and how large the gap can be. We now ask
whether those conditions arise for run energies derived from public timetables.

\paragraph{Data and instance construction.}
The dataset of \citet{sistig2025dataset} (CC BY 4.0) contains timetables, stops
and directed deadhead matrices for German bus operators. For each of the 20
operators in the archive we choose the day type with the most service records
after the speed screen, then retain trips whose endpoints appear in the
deadhead matrix. Trips departing in the morning peak $[05{:}30,09{:}30)$ form the A batch
and trips departing in the afternoon peak $[14{:}30,18{:}30)$ form the B batch.
Within each batch one maximum matching on the reachability graph partitions
trips into a minimum number of vehicle runs. Ties between minimum-run covers
may give different run energies. We condition on the stored cover. Each run's energy uses
the source study's vehicle parameters: 1.2\,kWh/km in service, 0.96\,kWh/km
deadheading, and 16\,kWh/h of auxiliary load away from the depot, including
pull-out and pull-in using separately chosen minimum-energy depot legs
\citep{sistig2025electrification}. We
exclude 108 trip records whose implied speed exceeds the 90\,km/h screen and the two
operators with fewer than five runs per batch. This leaves 18 operators with
6--413 morning runs and 6--298 afternoon runs. The 3913 run energies have
median 154\,kWh and interdecile range 110--204\,kWh. We retain these modeled
run energies, impose common charging windows and allow complete cross-batch
pairing. The resulting dispatches describe peak-run abstractions. Full-day
service, crews, consistent depot assignments and alternative within-peak
chainings require separate validation.

\paragraph{Economics.} We treat economics as a sensitivity grid rather than a
calibration. The daily vehicle cost is $f=150$\,EUR in the sweeps; critical
spreads scale linearly in $f$. The supply cost in each window is quadratic,
$\bar p_wE+\beta E^2/2$, so the marginal-cost spread is
$g(E)=s_0+\beta(2E-S_0)$, which is Lipschitz with $\kappa=2\beta$. The zero-load
spread $s_0=\bar p_E-\bar p_L$ ranges over 37 values from $-0.2$ to
$1.6$\,EUR/kWh. The price impact is $\beta=\rho\,\bar p/S_0$ with $\bar p=0.15$\,EUR/kWh
and $\rho\in\{0.5,1,2,4\}$: drawing the fleet's whole daily energy in one window
would raise that window's marginal cost by $\rho\bar p$. We report costs relative
to the total daily cost, vehicles plus energy, with the overnight zero-load price
set to $\bar p$. The midday budget $K$ is either ample ($K=P$) or half that, and
the overnight budget is ample. The model has no in-service charging, so a run
needing more than the usable battery $B$ cannot be served. We exclude such
operator--battery combinations rather than trimming their runs (57 runs exceed
250\,kWh, 13 exceed 300\,kWh, 8 exceed 350\,kWh, none exceed 400\,kWh).

\paragraph{Which operators admit a zero-requirement matching?}
\Cref{prop:anatomy}(i) turns the data into a necessary condition: a gap requires
a marginal spread of at least $f/\eta_{\max}$, which depends only on run energies
and battery size. \Cref{fig:critical} shows it per operator. With $B=400$\,kWh,
15 of 18 operators admit a maximum-size matching with zero mandatory midday
charge. Removing pairs gives $r_m=0$ at every smaller size. The cheapest
fleet therefore attains the entire feasible load interval. Only six of the
18 satisfy the stronger condition that \emph{every individual edge} has zero
requirement. For the three operators with a positive matching requirement the critical spread is
0.53\,EUR/kWh or more, and above 35\,EUR/kWh for two of them. Smaller batteries
make forced charging real. At $B=350$\,kWh, 5 of 17 eligible operators admit a gap,
from 0.81\,EUR/kWh (median 1.40). At $B=300$\,kWh, 8 of 14 do, from 0.91\,EUR/kWh
(median 1.27). At $B=250$\,kWh, all 9 eligible operators do, from 0.72\,EUR/kWh
(median 1.70). These thresholds are several times the 0.15\,EUR/kWh reference
energy price. The thresholds describe the marginal spreads a proposed supply
and fee model must reach at a feasible load. Medians here are conditional
on operators with a positive matching increment at the specified capacity.

\section{Duty commitment on a German distribution feeder}\label{sec:feeder}

\paragraph{Matched design.}
The primary design has 72 assignments: two German price days (17 January and
17 July 2024), two depot sites, three charger sizes, two matched fleet
classes, and three supply rules. The heterogeneous fleet resamples published
Euskirchen peak-run energies by fixed midpoint ranks to 5--51 services per
batch. The homogeneous control uses the same batch means. Both have 320\,kWh
usable batteries, efficiency 0.95, 125\,kW plugs, a common one-hour midday
window and a two-hour terminal window. These are constructed depot-scale
schedules with complete successor compatibility.

We use the native radial SimBench rural medium-voltage feeder
\citep{meinecke2020simbench}. Each 15-minute snapshot retains native load and
renewable profiles, taps, switches, shunts, voltage limits and terminal
current ratings. SimBench's 2016 same-week/weekday profiles proxy the two
2024 operating dates; Energinet records are explicitly filtered to the DE
price area and billed at their German delivery hour. A branch-flow conic
relaxation \citep{farivar2013branch} supplies numerical lower estimates;
independent pandapower Newton flows check constructed whole-bus upper costs.
No exactness theorem is presumed for this feeder.

Primary supply has hard native limits and no substitute supply. Two
sensitivities allow a 1\,MW unit at the depot at 414.7235 or
510.5304\,EUR/MWh, linear fuel-cost proxies from a published 1\,MW diesel
unit and Germany's 2024 average diesel price
\citep{cat3512,adac2024diesel}. These are scenarios, not local
bids or valuations of unserved service. A single constant depot MW per
window is realized by plug-sharing with variable power, pausing and migration.
We compare a predetermined rank pairing, an energy-aware minimum-bus
matching chosen before prices, joint physical scheduling and convexification.

\paragraph{Cost brackets and numerical limits.}
The primary comparison resolves 66/72 assignments within one cent: the
minimum-bus matching admits a whole-fleet schedule whose independently
computed AC cost matches the numerical hull lower estimate. Four conic
hulls are reported infeasible; two retain numerical bound widths of about
0.70 and 0.81\,EUR. These are tolerance-based computations, with unverified
exact-arithmetic dual feasibility. The first conic integer-count selection
passed all strict checks in only three assignments. Its original statuses
remain intact. Physical schedule construction and a second conic backend
provide the separate brackets reported here.

The secondary one-hour terminal comparison resolves all 30 assignments
with a numerical hull point; six are solver-infeasible. The additional
36 one-hour heterogeneous assignments fail a per-bus terminal charging
condition. They are a design limitation and are excluded from the primary
headline. Earlier residual failures are retained. Before date runs we
replaced an unstable pure relative cone screen by a declared absolute-plus-relative
screen in physical units, with independent AC agreement and native limits.
Five repaired Gurobi primals still fail that cone screen. A relaxation
can nevertheless bound a separately constructed physical point; solver
status alone is never used as a physical witness.

\paragraph{Binding limits without extra buses.}
At the largest far depot, the hard-supply hull is solver-infeasible for
both fleet classes on both days. With local backup the same 51-bus duties
are AC-feasible and match the hull lower estimates within one cent.
All twelve quarters reach the depot's native 0.965\,p.u. floor; no native
thermal constraint binds. Backup energy is 2.471\,MWh in January and
2.241\,MWh in July. Both window marginal prices equal the active backup
offer within numerical tolerance. The absolute price rises, but the spread
needed to buy an extra bus does not. The full wholesale ranges, 71.05 and
177.15\,EUR/MWh, are also below the original homogeneous fixture's
527.78\,EUR/MWh threshold. In the 36 distinct feasible wholesale comparisons,
all choose minimum fleet size. A predetermined pairing costs 0.40--6.54\,EUR
more in six January designs; the strong classical matching captures this
benefit before prices are observed.

\paragraph{Actual price updates.}
We freeze and run price $\to$ fleet response $\to$ fixed-load OPF $\to$
price on the original 73 admitted hull assignments, including 51 primary
assignments. All fleet responses use the complete count projection. Four
rules start at the wholesale window prices: steps 1, 0.5 and 0.1 and
$1/(k+1)$, with at most 100 iterations. Every grid price requires the
physical conic screen and independent AC agreement. For steps 1, 0.5 and
the diminishing rule, 43/73 reach a numerical fixed point; eight leave grid
feasibility and 22 fail grid admission. Primary counts are 27/51 fixed,
eight infeasible responses and 16 unresolved grid checks. With step 0.1,
16/51 reach the fixed-point test, eleven exhaust the iteration budget,
and the same eight and sixteen fail feasibility and admission. A small damped
price change alone is insufficient. Slow contracting sequences triggered
an overly permissive approximate-recurrence detector for step 0.1; we retain
those histories, withdraw their cycle labels and complete available traces
using only cached grid responses. No nontrivial cycle is established.
Prespecified flags cover positive support gap, price ambiguity, fleet ties
and a verified step-size mechanism. Unresolved paths are not assigned a
cause. Local two-sided price probes are recorded, with infeasible sides and
solver failures retained. These results give no empirical support for the
hypothesis of frequent cycles among the admitted fixed points.

\paragraph{Annual capacity and connection stakes.}
E-regio Netz serves Euskirchen. Its 2026 medium-voltage annual capacity
rates are 12.22 and 88.64\,EUR/kW-year below and above 2,500 utilization
hours, respectively; its one-time connection contribution is
95.64\,EUR/kW \citep{eregio2026network,eregio2026bkz}.
The 53.63--549.04\,kWh spread in mandatory early charging across pairings
is not automatically a reduction in the depot's peak. For window lengths
one and two hours, minimum peak is
$\min_{E\in[\ell,U]}\max\{E,(S-E)/2\}$ in kW when energies are kWh.
Every feasible matching at minimum fleet size admits the balanced point
$E=S/3$ in all six
primary heterogeneous designs and their homogeneous controls. Their minimum
peaks are identical, ranging from 453 to 4,660\,kW. The demonstrated annual
capacity and connection savings from choosing between these covers are
therefore zero in this fleet projection. Feeder feasibility and backup
requirements are separate from that minimum-power calculation. Repeating these three-hour windows every day gives at most
1,095 utilization hours. Other depot loads, annual peaks and contracted
connection capacity would be needed to value a different design.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{A decision at the depot.} Given a proposed bus fee and a specified
supplier cost curve, a planner can calculate the interval endpoints before
choosing a coordination rule. Outside the open failure bands, a feasible
fleet attains the hull optimum. At a supported tie the supplier can specify
the desired quantity in addition to the price. Inside a band, let $x_D$ be a
physical optimum. At its own marginal price the fleet can save at least
$\Delta$ by changing schedules. At a hull price $p^*$, the supplier can pay
the operator $r(x_D;p^*)$ conditional on operating $x_D$. The payment
is bounded above by $\Delta$ (\cref{prop:support}). This compares two
specified payment rules. Contract enforcement costs, participation rights
and observed tariff parameters are needed to value a change in practice.

The new feeder study supports representative matching within its two-window
class, while the hourly boundary test prevents a general recommendation to
fix arbitrary zero-price duties.

The study illustrates why a ratio alone is insufficient for that decision.
At 400\,kWh, regrets in the seven positive grid cells are several times
their planning gaps, yet the largest modeled saving from deviating is
4.56\,EUR per day. At 300\,kWh, counting affected operators on the grid
misses four operators with narrow failure bands. The interval calculation
identifies both the exposed operators and the range of offsets to check.

\paragraph{For price-coordination algorithms.} \Cref{thm:iteration} separates
two causes of non-convergence. On a flat face a supplier that knows the
target load can request it at the supporting spread. A tie-breaking rule
applied only at that spread cannot alter a trajectory that never reaches
it. An undamped cycle may also surround a supported vertex. Under diminishing
steps prices converge, and for an affine spread the step-weighted loads and
fees recover the hull point. A supplier can distinguish these cases by
checking whether a feasible schedule attains the target load \emph{and}
the hull vehicle cost.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The exact theorem assumes two synchronized batches,
common availability and efficiencies, zero-time transitions, ideal switching
and period-aggregate settlement. \Cref{sec:boundaries} shows that
edge-dependent transitions and a third batch are genuine boundaries, and that
setup time requires separate treatment. The study derives run energies from
public timetables and assumed energy rates. Estimating savings for a depot
would require observed consumption, duty options and settlement terms.
The 37-service certificate establishes
existence beyond the class for one timetable with synthetic economics.

\paragraph{Open directions.} Further work beyond the present tests could examine: a
characterization for staggered charging windows, where serialization is no
longer automatic, a measured application of the setup-time bound, and an
analysis of a strategic fleet operator who anticipates the price change.
