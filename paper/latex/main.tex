\section*{Proposed abstract}
Discrete service assignments, battery states, charging windows, shared chargers, and terminal replenishment can prevent an hourly marginal price from supporting a physically feasible electric-bus schedule, even with smooth convex supply costs. We compare complete feasible fleet plans with the convex relaxation over their joint operating-cost and load vectors. In a fully replenished two-service construction, physical and convexified costs are 97 and 94.8875; at a common hull price, fleet lost opportunity is zero while supplier lost opportunity is 2.1125, and the fleet's own-price regret is 13. A robustness variant retains positive nonconvexity with a reserve, 5\% charging loss, and one finite connector. In a scaling family, the absolute planning gap tends to zero even as whole-operator regret can remain positive, whereas per-bus and individually reserved-pair incentives vanish. For one 37-service, depot-15 stored-input instance, an exact rational witness and one-bus obstruction establish a two-bus minimum. An independently reconstructed, off-protocol nonlinear diagnostic leaves the public gap unresolved; its positive own-price regret concerns a bounded planner incumbent, not a proven optimum.

\section{Strategic price impact is a separate model}
A price-taking fleet chooses among feasible plans while treating the posted
hourly price vector as fixed during its deviation. This tests whether that
marginal tariff decentralizes the plan, even though the price itself is
determined by aggregate load. For a strategic fleet, the settlement rule
matters: a bill of $c(x)+p(e(x))\cdot e(x)$ makes the fleet internalize how its
load changes the price, whereas a system planner minimizes $c(x)+G(e(x))$.
Even when the supply curve is known, the former can be a one-level strategic
bill optimization; it is not automatically the same model as price-taking
support. Convex-hull or uplift bounds for price-taking do not measure market
power or strategic surplus. Decentralized price coordination is a related but
distinct question (\citep{ganTopcuLow2013}); strategic-market and nonconvex
pricing formulations also differ in their objectives
(\citep{oneill2005,gribikHoganPope2007}).
