\begin{abstract}
Electric-bus charging combines continuous energy decisions with indivisible service assignments. We ask whether a feasible fleet schedule is a best response to the marginal electricity prices generated by its own load. An exact, fully replenished two-service construction has a positive gap between physical and convexified planning, which persists with charging losses, a reserve and a finite connector. Replication can make the absolute planning gap vanish while whole-operator regret remains positive along a subsequence; regret per reserved pair vanishes. For two depot variants of a public timetable, a fleet-cardinality and energy argument gives analytical lower bounds stronger than the reported iterative bounds. Joint reporting of physical cost, hull cost and own-price regret shows what existing computations establish and what remains unresolved. The public intervals do not establish a positive planning gap, and regret of a returned incumbent does not establish regret at an optimal fleet. These distinctions guide further computational evaluation.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} electric-bus scheduling; convex-hull pricing; marginal tariffs; charging coordination; nonconvex scheduling.

\section{Introduction}
An electric-bus operator must cover mandatory passenger services while assigning vehicles and scheduling charging. A tariff can shift charging toward cheaper periods, but a fleet large enough to affect aggregate electricity demand also changes the marginal cost behind that tariff. Even with continuously adjustable charging, its service assignments remain indivisible. The resulting question is whether a physically realizable fleet schedule minimizes the operator's bill when evaluated at the marginal prices associated with that schedule's load.

We study this question using complete fleet plans. Each plan includes all services, vehicle movements, charging and operating cost. Convexifying their joint cost and load vectors provides a useful planning lower bound, but a point in that hull is generally not an executable daily dispatch. Charging a convex supply cost at the mean load also differs from taking the mean cost of randomized physical dispatches. Maintaining these distinctions is essential both for interpreting a pricing failure and for assessing computational shortcuts.

The contribution has two parts. First, a small continuous-charging construction makes the obstruction explicit under full terminal replenishment. A perturbation permits a positive reserve, charging losses and one finite connector. A replication calculation then separates an aggregate planning gap from the incentives of a whole-fleet operator and of participants with individually reserved resources. These are application-specific exact results within established nonconvex pricing theory. Second, an energy argument strengthens the public timetable's lower bounds, and existing computations are organized around physical planning cost, hull cost and own-price regret. This makes the computational evidence answer the same question as the analytical model.

The computational scope is two small synthetic diagnostics and two depot variants of one public timetable. The public bounds remain open; they do not establish a positive nonconvexity gap or the absence of a supporting physical plan on that timetable. The main paper reports those limits alongside the exact mechanism. A separate development supplement preserves exploratory reuse and solver-start comparisons, whose budgets, incomplete intervention records and software failures prevent general acceleration claims. Broader computational validation remains part of the research program.

\section{Implications and limits}
The analytical and computational parts answer complementary questions. The construction proves that continuous charging and full replenishment do not remove the possibility of a marginal-price support failure. The experiments measure how difficult it is to obtain informative full-fleet bounds on declared instances. An unresolved public interval neither confirms the analytical mechanism on that timetable nor refutes it. Likewise, a strong restricted-master solution says little about the cost of discovering an ungenerated physical fleet unless global pricing has supplied a corresponding bound.

The analytical energy floor is a baseline that any computational method should use when bounding this model. A weak solver lower bound should not displace it. Strengthening physical pricing remains useful for narrowing global intervals, but the underlying cardinality and energy inequalities must be translated into each pricing model with their assumptions intact. A lower bound on the nonlinear hull objective is not by itself a valid cut on every linear price-response objective.

The exploratory comparisons in the development supplement do not establish general acceleration. On a three-service case, cold runs stopped at call or arithmetic limits well before their wall-time allowance; success from a retained pool is therefore conditional on those caps. Public solver-start comparisons used one seed and did not establish that submitted starts were accepted. Future experiments should size algorithmic limits before fixing the evaluation design, log start acceptance, compare time to common solution quality across repeated seeds, and include independent timetables. Retained complete fleets and nearest-neighbor retrieval should precede learned proposals, with direct proposal quality, physical repair and global verification reported separately. All variants of a base timetable must stay in one data group, with independent test timetables reserved before training.

Failure of a particular price-update iteration would not itself prove that no supporting price exists. Such convergence experiments must retain the same physical model, including terminal replenishment, and must be distinguished from the support and regret statements proved here.

The physical model abstracts from stochastic delays, battery degradation, temperature-dependent consumption, nonlinear charging taper and operational switching times. Public service and travel inputs are combined with declared energy and economic assumptions; the results are not estimates of savings for the source operator. Resource rights also delimit the economic interpretation. A whole-fleet price-taking deviation, an individually reserved-pair deviation and a strategic operator anticipating its own price impact are different optimization problems. Conclusions must follow the chosen participant and settlement model.

\section{Conclusion}
A smooth convex electricity supply cost need not support an indivisible electric-bus fleet at its own marginal prices, even with continuous charging and full terminal replenishment. Replication can make the planning gap small without making the incentive of a whole-fleet operator small along every subsequence, while incentives for reserved pairs vanish. Analytical cardinality and energy bounds strengthen the public computations. Joint physical, hull and regret reporting leaves public price support unresolved and separates incumbent incentives from optimal-fleet incentives. Establishing the mechanism's practical prevalence and an effective computational method requires further independent timetable evidence.
