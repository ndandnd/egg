\section{Related work}

Electric-bus scheduling combines discrete service and vehicle assignments with battery and charging decisions. Wu et al. study a multi-depot bus-scheduling problem with time-of-use tariffs and peak-load objectives, solved with branch-and-price and trip-chain reuse \citep{wu2022grid}. De Vos et al. include partial charging and limited station capacity in a path-based public-transit formulation \citep{devos2024capacity}; Parmentier et al. develop scalable route and recharge scheduling by column generation for electric fleets serving passenger requests \citep{parmentier2023fleets}. These works establish the operational role of duty construction, energy limits, and charging resources. The question here is different: how a complete feasible fleet plan relates to the convex hull of complete plans under a common convex supply-cost model. A relaxation whose columns are individual vehicle duties need not equal this whole-fleet hull, and a mean load in the hull need not be executable as one day's bus schedule.

Public timetable and movement data make this distinction testable, while leaving modeling choices visible. Sistig et al. generate vehicle and crew schedules for twenty German transport networks from public GTFS inputs; their accompanying versioned dataset includes routes, trips, stops, possible deadheads, and schedules \citep{sistig2025dataset,sistig2025electrification}. The present public intake uses one Hildenbrand timetable in two restricted depot variants. Source distances and times are combined with declared energy, battery, charger, and synthetic supply-cost assumptions; the source supplies reference bus parameters, while EGG's reserve, charger, horizon and convex supply-cost assumptions remain declared scenario choices. It supplies no EGG market-supply function. The reviewed computational campaigns remain small development studies on this one timetable and its two depots. Their public global bounds remain open, and they establish neither an equal-quality speedup nor a learned-proposal benefit.

Nonconvex electricity-market research explains why marginal energy prices can fail to support a dispatch selected with indivisible operating decisions. Gribik et al. compare pricing rules for unit commitment and dispatch and discuss the energy uplift needed when no energy-price vector supports the selected quantities \citep{gribikHoganPope2007}. O'Neill et al. develop prices from a mixed-integer model and an associated linear program for nonconvex markets \citep{oneill2005}. Convex-hull pricing then studies uniform prices through convexification: Schiro et al. explain its formulation and implementation issues, while Hua and Baldick give a convex primal formulation for unit-commitment hull pricing \citep{schiro2016,huaBaldick2017}. We use this established convexification and supporting-price logic for a stated fleet set and supply-cost function. The resulting own-price best-response test is conditional on that common model; it is neither a general market-clearing theorem nor a settlement design. In particular, fleet regret at its own marginal price is not an uplift payment under any of these market rules.

Aggregation results also require their own scale and participant definitions. Starr's classical quasi-equilibrium result gives an aggregation perspective for economies with nonconvex preferences \citep{starr1969}. Our replication construction is proved directly for its specified fleet schedules: the aggregate planning gap tends to zero while whole-operator regret can remain positive, whereas per-bus and individually reserved-pair incentives vanish. A Shapley--Folkman aggregation argument does not by itself prove this exact replication result or force the absolute regret of one composite operator to vanish. The distinction is between aggregating many separately bounded participants and enlarging a single participant whose opportunity set remains indivisible.

Price coordination for flexible EV charging is likewise established. Gan et al. formulate each vehicle's charging profile as a continuous, rate-bounded vector with a fixed energy requirement over its availability window, and give a decentralized price-update protocol that converges for a convex aggregate-load objective \citep{ganTopcuLow2013}. That framework coordinates charging once availability and energy needs are specified. Electric-bus schedules also choose which trips connect to which vehicles; those discrete choices alter each bus's charging windows and the joint feasible load set. Convex coordination over charging profiles therefore does not imply that a discrete complete bus schedule is supported by its own marginal price.

Finally, fixed-schedule fleet counting has a classical analogue in Dantzig and Fulkerson's minimum-tanker problem for meeting a prescribed schedule \citep{dantzigFulkerson1954}. Fixed-schedule fleet-count reductions address how many vehicles cover fixed movements. By themselves, they do not certify battery-state feasibility, shared-charger feasibility, or price support. We keep the minimum-fleet flow diagnostic separate from complete-schedule charging replay and from the economic comparison between physical plans and their convex hull.
