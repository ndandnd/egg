\section{Related work}

Electric-bus scheduling jointly assigns service to vehicles and tracks battery and charging decisions. Wu et al. study multi-depot scheduling with grid objectives; de Vos et al. model partial charging and station capacity; Parmentier et al. scale route and recharge scheduling by column generation \citep{wu2022grid,devos2024capacity,parmentier2023fleets}. These works establish operational roles for duty construction, energy limits, and charging resources. L\"obel et al. model nonlinear charging with variable recharge rates, showing why charging approximations require care \citep{lobel2024charging}. Here we ask how a complete fleet plan relates to the convex hull of complete plans under a common convex supply-cost model. A duty-column relaxation need not equal this whole-fleet hull, and a mean load in the hull need not be executable as one day's bus schedule.

Aggregating charging flexibility into a market bid is an established application. Zoltowska and Lin use bus-specific availability and energy inputs, mixed-integer aggregation and disaggregation, and a linear hourly auction representation \citep{zoltowskaLin2021}. Our distinction is the additional choice of discrete service assignments and whole-fleet operating cost: support of an aggregate charging allocation does not automatically settle support over those alternatives. This is a scope distinction, not a claim that their approach ignores discrete charging constraints. Public timetable data from Sistig et al. provide operational inputs for testing such questions \citep{sistig2025dataset,sistig2025electrification}.

Nonconvex electricity-market research explains why marginal prices can fail to support dispatch with indivisible operating decisions. Gribik et al. compare unit-commitment pricing rules and discuss energy uplift when no energy-price vector supports selected quantities \citep{gribikHoganPope2007}; O'Neill et al. construct prices from a mixed-integer model and associated linear program \citep{oneill2005}. Convex-hull pricing studies uniform prices through convexification: Schiro et al. discuss its formulation and implementation, and Hua and Baldick give a convex primal formulation \citep{schiro2016,huaBaldick2017}. Madani et al. compare convex-hull, integer-programming, and European-style pricing with nonconvex demand bids; Andrianesis et al. compute convex-hull prices by Dantzig--Wolfe decomposition and column generation \citep{madani2018pricing,andrianesis2022hull}. Hümbs et al. characterize linear-price support for decentralized problems with integralities and complete linear feasible-set descriptions \citep{huembs2022complete}. Bichler et al. study pricing with flexible demand in nonconvex electricity markets and the tradeoffs between pricing objectives \citep{bichler2023demand}. These are precedents for convex-hull pricing and price-support questions; we use the same general supporting-price logic for a stated fleet set and supply-cost function. Our own-price best-response test is conditional on that model, not a general market-clearing theorem or settlement design; fleet regret is not an uplift payment under these market rules.

Aggregation results require a scale and a participant definition. Starr's quasi-equilibrium result gives the classical perspective for nonconvex economies \citep{starr1969}. Hreinsson et al. apply Shapley--Folkman reasoning to the action sets and costs of aggregated dispatchable demand and develop reduced convex representations for system operators \citep{hreinsson2021}. Our replication calculation is explicit: its planning gap tends to zero while a whole operator's absolute regret can stay positive along a subsequence, although per-bus and reserved-pair incentives vanish. Approximate convexity of an aggregate does not, by itself, bound the absolute deviation gain of a composite participant.

Alizadeh et al. couple transportation and power networks using continuous traffic path flows, generator dispatch, locational prices and dual decomposition \citep{alizadeh2016coupled}. Scaglione derives marginal prices for generation trajectories with intertemporal ramp constraints \citep{scaglione2016marginal}. These motivate our generation-side dispatch extension and price-coordination interpretation. Their convex flow or dispatch formulations do not establish support for an atomic fleet with discrete service assignments. Gan et al. likewise coordinate continuous EV charging profiles with fixed availability and energy requirements \citep{ganTopcuLow2013}; bus assignments additionally change charging windows and the joint feasible load set. We examine what survives this change in the participant's feasible set, rather than attributing generic nonconvergence to those convex coordination methods.

Fixed-schedule fleet counting has a classical analogue in Dantzig and Fulkerson's minimum-tanker problem \citep{dantzigFulkerson1954}. Such flow reductions count vehicles covering fixed movements, but do not certify battery states, shared-charger feasibility, or price support. We separate minimum-fleet flow diagnostics, charging replay, and convex-hull economic comparisons.
