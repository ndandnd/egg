# Modern ML methods and validation for route proposals

## Recommendation

Continue with interpretable tabular baselines first, then add a small boosted-tree comparison. The current eight-group pilot has only eight independent timetable groups, and its edge labels are sparse observations from source-solver incumbents. At a fixed 0.5 threshold, all four pilot methods predicted every edge negative; the MLP's Brier score improved slightly over the movement-kind frequency control, while its log loss did not. That is not enough evidence to promote a neural model or claim route-quality benefit.

For the next model comparison, retain the prevalence and movement-kind controls, and replace the fixed-epoch logistic implementation with a converged L2-regularized logistic baseline. Use a small, declared regularization grid and fit all preprocessing within each training fold. Add one boosted-tree implementation, preferably CatBoost for its ordered handling of categorical features, or XGBoost if its existing runtime/support is more practical. The CatBoost and XGBoost papers establish credible methods and describe their own benchmark results; neither establishes a current winner or expected performance on this dataset ([CatBoost](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2018/hash/14491b756b3a51daac41c24863285549-Abstract.html), [XGBoost](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.02754)). Keep depth, leaf size, learning rate, and number of rounds conservative. If tuning rounds, use only inner grouped folds, never the outer held groups.

Treat the eight-group comparison as an engineering pilot. Keep all rows, both source variants, charging variants, and repeated observations from one `base_group` together in a fold. Grouped folds prevent group overlap by design ([GroupKFold documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.GroupKFold.html)). With so few independent groups, use a tiny prespecified search, report each group's result, average repeated seeds within group before averaging across groups, and avoid treating thousands of correlated edge rows as independent replications. Model-selection scores can themselves be overfit when many alternatives are tried on a small sample, so keep the model menu and selection rule fixed before evaluation ([Cawley and Talbot, 2010](https://jmlr.csail.mit.edu/beta/papers/v11/cawley10a.html)). Continue the registered 32/64/128-group checkpoints and preserve the sealed development/test groups for a later, one-time evaluation.

Because positive edges are rare, prioritize held-group log loss and Brier score alongside PR-AUC, groupwise positive recall at a prespecified candidate budget, and calibration. Report the prevalence-only and kind-frequency controls. Fix any operational threshold or top-k budget using training-only grouped validation, then freeze it before evaluating held groups. Accuracy at 0.5 is not useful here: the pilot's 93.88% accuracy was the all-negative prediction. Do not use held-group outcomes to select a model, threshold, seed, or feature set.

## Label and feature semantics

The target is “appears in a physically replayed source incumbent,” not “belongs to an optimal fleet.” An unchosen edge is unlabeled with respect to feasibility and quality: it may be feasible, may appear in another incumbent, or may be part of a better fleet. Keep the current label meaning explicit in every model receipt and report. Do not call these edges infeasible or train them as verified bad moves. If future labels compare alternatives, derive preferences only from independently evaluated fleet-level outcomes with a prespecified cost tolerance; do not assign edge-level blame from a fleet-level cost difference.

Keep predictors prospective: frozen case/movement attributes and source-market data available before target solving. Exclude selected-edge bits, source-plan topology, target charging schedules, costs, hull bounds, solver statuses, receipts, and all group/source IDs or hashes. The model may rank candidate edges, but only a constrained decoder should construct a route/fleet proposal. Record ranking, decoding, physical replay, and verification as separate stages and charge their elapsed time separately.

## Neural combinatorial optimization: later, as a constrained proposal method

The neural literature is a useful design reference once there are many more independent training groups and a route-aware evaluation loop. [Kool, van Hoof, and Welling (ICLR 2019)](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=ByxBFsRqYm) train an attention policy for TSP, VRP, Orienteering, and prize-collecting TSP variants; [Joshi, Laurent, and Bresson (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.01227) use a graph convolutional edge scorer and beam search for Euclidean TSP. These papers motivate attention/graph representations and structured search, but their benchmark results do not transfer directly to this task.

Two newer candidates are more relevant architectural references. [RouteFinder](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15007) combines a transformer with attribute-conditioned representations, mixed-variant training, and adapters across VRP variants; its [research repository](https://github.com/ai4co/routefinder) documents the implementation. [RRNCO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16159), accepted at ICLR 2026, models real asymmetric distance and duration matrices and edge features; its [official repository](https://github.com/ai4co/real-routing-nco) includes data and code for ATSP, capacitated VRP, and VRP with time windows. These are contemporary candidates for later experiments, not evidence that a neural method is state of the art on this EV timetable task. They still do not represent its complete service path-cover, battery-SOC, charging-window, and shared-grid constraints or its observed-incumbent supervision. Benchmark wins on VRP distributions do not establish a gain here.

The present problem is a timetable path-cover with depot starts/ends, service coverage, deadhead compatibility, vehicle-count objectives, battery state, charging opportunities, and shared charger/grid limits. Standard TSP/VRP benchmarks generally use different feasible sets, cost structures, and instance distributions. An edge scorer or attention decoder does not by itself enforce the present energy and charging constraints. Any later neural proposal must feed a feasibility-preserving decoder or optimizer, then pass the existing independent physical replay and target-market verification. Compare it against the same cost-only decoder and classical optimizer under matched time budgets; report feasibility, fleet cost, verification outcome, and full online wall time. A learned score or fast inference alone is not a route-quality or speedup result.

## Staged plan

1. **Now:** converged regularized logistic plus one conservative boosted-tree model, against prevalence/kind controls, under grouped folds and fixed small tuning budgets. Retain calibration and candidate-budget metrics; preserve the observed-incumbent label caveat.
2. **As labels grow:** repeat with the predetermined group prefixes and a nested grouped selection protocol. Freeze preprocessing, model family, hyperparameters, and candidate-budget rule before the sealed development evaluation. Track performance by independent base group and by source fleet; keep acquisition and inference/decoder/replay time separate.
3. **After route-level data support it:** test a graph/attention scorer or policy as a route-proposal method, not a replacement for feasibility checks or optimization. Add cost-only and classical solver controls, identical repair/replay budgets, independent feasibility verification, and a prespecified measure of target cost and wall time. Use later independent groups for the final comparison.

No new model was fit and no development/test data were accessed for this note. The recommendation is a staged plan, not a claim that any cited method is state of the art for timetable path-cover with charging.
